    MY WINNIPEG with C. Rees

    AFS · Film

    • Thu, Dec 10 · 6:30 PM
    • Sun, Dec 13 · 5:45 PM

    Guy Maddin’s handmade hallucination turns Winnipeg into a fever dream of memory.

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    Maddin turns documentary into a handmade cinematic hallucination, combining silent film textures, rear projection, intertitles, rapid dissolves, and aggressively stylized narration. The train journey, the stacked swimming pools, and the staged return to his childhood home show exceptional control of rhythm and visual association. The re-created family scenes, including the carpeted lump standing in for his dead father, make private memory into grotesque theatrical ritual. The film occasionally lets its verbal invention outrun its images, and the frantic editing can reduce individual shots to flashes, but its formal excess remains purposeful.

    ✨ Originality

    Few films treat civic history, autobiography, urban myth, and fabricated evidence as interchangeable material. Maddin’s Winnipeg is simultaneously a city, a family romance, a crime scene, and a film set. The recurring train, the invented local legends, the actors impersonating Maddin’s relatives, and the serial about a man repeatedly preparing to jump from a ledge create a structure that comments on its own repetitions. This is not merely an unusual documentary. It is a sustained argument that memory becomes real through performance.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    The film expands the documentary form by rejecting the usual contract between evidence and explanation. Its “docu-fantasia” treats regional identity as something built from hearsay, civic trauma, family mythology, and cinematic memory. Winnipeg is not presented as neutral geography. It becomes the psychological machinery that produces Maddin’s artistic identity. The film’s importance lies in showing how a local portrait can become a radical inquiry into national memory and the authority of images.

    💡 Intellectual Depth

    Maddin’s central question is whether filming can release him from the place that formed him. The answer remains unstable, because every attempted escape sends him back into reenactment, narration, and fantasy. The film’s deepest subject is not Winnipeg itself, but the way identity depends on repeated stories whose factual status no longer matters. The staged childhood episodes expose memory as both inheritance and direction, especially in the treatment of the dead sibling, the absent father, and the mother’s domestic power. Its ideas are complex without becoming solemn, though the density of its symbols sometimes produces accumulation rather than development. The result is an exceptional film, funny, difficult, and formally impossible to confuse with anything else.

  2. 02
    8 / 10

    TO LIVE AND DIE IN L.A.

    AFS · Film

    • Sat, Dec 12 · 5:45 PM

    Friedkin’s L.A. burns with speed, paper, ink, and controlled brutality.

    🎭 Artistic Merit. Friedkin builds the film from speed, surfaces, and controlled brutality. Robby Müller’s location photography turns Los Angeles into a bleaching, unstable grid, where pastel daylight, industrial lots, freeway concrete, and cheap interiors carry as much dramatic weight as dialogue. The counterfeit money sequences give the film a rare tactile focus, especially the close attention to paper, ink, heat, and chemical process. The freeway pursuit, moving through truck yards, the Los Angeles River, and oncoming traffic, remains a major achievement in action staging because geography stays legible while panic escalates. Wang Chung’s electronic score pushes the film toward music video form, sometimes at the expense of character, but it gives the picture a distinct pulse.

    ✨ Originality. The film’s strongest invention is its collision of police thriller, neo noir, and feverish eighties commercial imagery. Richard Chance is not a stable hero but a man whose professional identity has become appetite, and his pursuit of Eric Masters gradually makes them look like variations of the same criminal intelligence. The plot uses familiar undercover machinery, yet the film repeatedly rejects moral reassurance, particularly in its abrupt violence and its willingness to destroy the audience’s investment in conventional justice. Its style is occasionally excessive, but that excess is the point. The movie treats Los Angeles as a system of surfaces that can be forged, bought, or abruptly shattered.

    📚 Cultural Significance. The film helped define the hard, polished strain of American neo noir that treated the city as an economic and psychological environment rather than a backdrop. Its location shooting, pastel palette, aggressive editing, and Wang Chung soundtrack made it a key example of the period’s fusion between crime cinema and music video aesthetics. Its initial commercial performance was modest, but later reassessment established it as an overlooked eighties action film with lasting influence on discussions of urban style, professional corruption, and cinematic car chases.

    💡 Intellectual Depth. The film is most compelling when it links identity to imitation. Masters forges money, Chance performs righteousness, and the police bureaucracy converts violence into procedure. Everyone operates through copies, disguises, transactions, and rehearsed roles. The film’s coldness is both limitation and argument. It offers little emotional interiority, but that absence suits a world where desire has been reduced to acquisition and performance. The ending’s reversal does not redeem Chance or punish Masters in a simple moral scheme. It leaves a corrupt system intact, with individuals replaced as efficiently as counterfeit bills. The film sacrifices psychological richness for velocity and visual control, but its ideas survive because its form enacts them.

    CINEMA PARADISO

    AFS · Film

    • Sun, Dec 13 · 1:00 PM

    Tornatore's cinema-love story, Morricone's score, and a boy's projectionist memories.

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    Tornatore builds the film around a clean visual and emotional structure, moving from Salvatore’s adult life in Rome back through his childhood in Giancaldo. The projection booth, the village square, and the packed auditorium become precise social spaces, not mere backdrops. Ennio Morricone’s score gives the film its emotional architecture, while the editing links fragments of film history to Salvatore’s private memory. The 155 minute version also exposes the film’s weakness, its middle sections repeat familiar emotional beats and occasionally overstates their importance.

    ✨ Originality

    The film’s strongest invention is its use of censorship as narrative material. The priest’s bell announces each cut, turning suppressed kisses into a visual archive that later returns in Alfredo’s reel. The final montage converts those discarded fragments into a private inheritance, an unusually direct image of cinema recovering what institutions erase. The larger story, however, follows recognizable patterns, the gifted child, the wise mentor, the lost love, and the artist who must leave home. Its achievement lies less in plot innovation than in joining these conventions to the material life of projection, celluloid, and communal spectatorship.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    Cinema Paradiso helped establish the modern popular film about loving movies, presenting cinema as a village institution, a place for flirtation, gossip, class mixing, escape, and shared fantasy. Its international success, including the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film and the Cannes Grand Prix, made Tornatore a major international director and helped renew global interest in Italian cinema. The film’s influence is clearest in the familiar image of the cinema as a disappearing civic space, and in the widespread imitation of its kissing montage finale.

    💡 Intellectual Depth

    The film treats memory as both preservation and distortion. Adult Salvatore remembers the theater as a communal sanctuary, yet the story also shows poverty, censorship, war damage, religious authority, and the loneliness produced by ambition. Alfredo’s command that Salvatore leave Giancaldo is generous but severe, since artistic freedom requires abandoning the people and places that formed him. The final reel offers release without restoring the past. Its images survive only because they were cut away, making the film’s central argument concrete, cinema preserves experience by transforming it, not by returning it intact.

    7 / 10

    MARY OLIVER: SAVED BY THE BEAUTY OF THE WORLD

    AFS · Film

    • Mon, Dec 7 · 6:30 PM

    Oliver documentary builds rhythm from absence, pairing poems with images and voices.

    🎭 Artistic Merit. Sasha Waters builds a controlled, attractive documentary from a difficult absence. Mary Oliver left little filmed testimony, so the film relies on audio, photographs, letters, archival fragments, landscape imagery, and readings by figures including Stephen Colbert, John Waters, Helena Bonham Carter, and Steve Buscemi. The strongest formal choice is to let images react to Oliver’s poems rather than merely illustrate them. That method gives the film rhythm and atmosphere, though its polished visual language sometimes softens the harder facts of Oliver’s abuse, privacy, and difficult career.

    ✨ Originality. The film’s workaround for its limited archive is intelligent, but not radically new. Its collage of voices, nature footage, archival material, and celebrity readings suits Oliver’s plainspoken poetry, yet the structure remains a respectful literary biography. The film gains force from the tension between public admiration and private reserve, but it rarely risks an interpretation that might unsettle Oliver’s established reputation. Its originality is strongest in form, not argument.

    📚 Cultural Significance. The documentary makes a persuasive case for Oliver’s place in American literary culture by connecting her poems to readers, writers, performers, and the natural world she repeatedly examined. Its broad range of admirers reflects her unusual popular reach. It also addresses the contradiction between mass readership and limited academic recognition, including the absence of a full length New York Times review. The film contributes more to public access and cultural preservation than to revisionist scholarship, but that contribution is substantial.

    💡 Intellectual Depth. Waters treats Oliver’s privacy as both subject and formal problem. The film asks how to portray an artist who resisted exposure, and it answers by placing competing pressures beside one another, biography, literary celebrity, trauma, partnership, solitude, and the moral claims of attention to nature. Still, the documentary is more celebratory than analytical. It acknowledges criticism of Oliver’s popularity without examining it at an academic level, and its admirers considerably outnumber its skeptics. The result is thoughtful and well made, but not sufficiently argumentative to reach the exceptional range.

    JIMMY

    AFS · Film

    • Mon, Dec 7 · 7:00 PM

    Black-and-white 16mm Paris as sensory field, tracing young James Baldwin.

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    Yashaddai Owens gives JIMMY a clear visual identity through black and white 16mm photography and an impressionistic structure. The film treats Paris less as a historical backdrop than as a sensory field, with Benny O. Arthur’s young Baldwin moving through the city, its Algerian quarters, and its Left Bank artistic circles. The short running time, listed as 61 minutes, supports the film’s concentrated form, though the emphasis on atmosphere leaves some dramatic development thin. The result is controlled and visually assured, but not fully substantial as narrative cinema.

    ✨ Originality

    The film’s strongest choice is to approach Baldwin through perception rather than conventional biography. Owens imagines his arrival in Paris as an encounter with new forms of racial, erotic, and artistic freedom, avoiding a chronological account of literary achievement. That strategy gives the film unpredictability and formal energy. Its weakness is that the familiar artist in exile framework remains recognizable, and the impressionistic method sometimes substitutes suggestion for discovery.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    JIMMY contributes to the screen’s portrait of James Baldwin by focusing on the young writer before his public stature was fixed. Its attention to Baldwin’s alienation, sexuality, and escape from American racism connects his Paris years to larger questions of artistic migration and self-invention. The film’s contribution is primarily interpretive rather than historical, since it reimagines Baldwin’s experience instead of documenting it.

    💡 Intellectual Depth

    The film’s central idea is that artistic identity can emerge through changed surroundings. Paris becomes a testing ground where Baldwin observes, desires, and absorbs a world less immediately governed by the pressures he left behind. The approach is intellectually promising because it links geography, erotic freedom, racial estrangement, and literary formation. Still, the film offers an evocative premise more successfully than a fully developed argument. Its complexity lies in its method and subject, not always in its character writing.

    Overall judgment:

    A strong, visually distinctive first feature with genuine formal ambition. Its achievement is the creation of a subjective Baldwin portrait, while its limitation is the narrow distance between impressionistic insight and underdeveloped drama.

    MILLION DOLLAR MERMAID

    AFS · Film

    • Tue, Dec 8 · 7:30 PM
    • Sat, Dec 12 · 3:00 PM

    Esther Williams swims through Busby Berkeley’s Technicolor water ballets.

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    Mervyn LeRoy’s film is a polished MGM spectacle, built around Esther Williams’s athletic control and George Folsey’s Technicolor cinematography. Busby Berkeley’s water choreography supplies the strongest passages, especially the Hippodrome sequence with towering fountains, colored smoke, and massed swimmers arranged as moving geometry. Williams carries the film physically, turning dives, underwater formations, and synchronized movement into character expression. The dramatic scenes are less assured. Victor Mature’s broad romantic performance and the episodic screenplay reduce Annette Kellerman’s ambition to a series of publicity crises and reconciliations.

    ✨ Originality

    The film’s story is conventional, but its aquatic staging remains unusually inventive. Swimming, ballet, vaudeville, fashion controversy, and circus display are fused into one production language rather than treated as separate attractions. The one piece bathing suit functions as both plot device and visual manifesto, making public scandal part of the spectacle. Still, the film repeatedly retreats into familiar romance, comic relief, and biographical simplification. Its originality belongs to the form of the water numbers, not to its narrative design.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    Million Dollar Mermaid helped define Esther Williams’s screen identity and stands as one of the clearest examples of the Hollywood aquatic musical. It also converts Kellerman’s challenge to restrictive public standards into a commercially safe star vehicle. The film’s historical value lies in that contradiction, it presents female physical independence while packaging it for consumption through glamour, romance, and spectacle. As biography, it is loose. As evidence of early 1950s studio cinema’s ability to monumentalize the female body, it is substantial.

    💡 Intellectual Depth

    The film has limited psychological complexity, but it contains a useful conflict between athletic achievement and entertainment value. Kellerman wants bodily freedom and artistic legitimacy, while promoters turn those goals into marketable sensation. The Boston bathing suit episode makes that tension explicit, her body becomes controversial precisely because it is functional rather than ornamental. The screenplay does not develop this idea consistently, and the romance repeatedly displaces it. The result is a strong visual argument weakened by a thin biographical structure.

    WHITE CHRISTMAS

    Paramount · Film

    • Wed, Dec 9 · 7:00 PM
    • Sun, Dec 13 · 7:00 PM

    Bing Crosby and Danny Kaye sing through a polished, artificial Technicolor holiday.

    🎭 🎭 Artistic Merit

    Michael Curtiz directs White Christmas with efficient studio polish, using Technicolor, VistaVision framing, and tightly staged musical numbers to give the film scale without sacrificing clarity. The strongest sequence is “Sisters,” where synchronized movement and theatrical symmetry turn a deliberately artificial performance into a sharp comic device. Bing Crosby supplies relaxed vocal control, Danny Kaye brings elastic physical timing, and Rosemary Clooney provides the film’s most grounded presence. The plot is mechanically assembled, but the production remains precise and entertaining.

    ✨ ✨ Originality

    The film is not especially innovative. Its story, two entertainers, two sisters, romantic misunderstandings, and a threatened Vermont inn, follows established musical comedy formulas. Its best invention lies in the tension between backstage performance and private feeling. “The Best Things Happen While You’re Dancing” and “Count Your Blessings Instead of Sheep” use song not merely as decoration, but as a way to expose courtship, anxiety, and emotional restraint. The film’s originality is therefore formal and performative, not narrative.

    📚 📚 Cultural Significance

    Its cultural importance is substantial. Irving Berlin’s “White Christmas” already ranked as an exceptionally successful American popular song before the film, and the movie made it the organizing emblem of a postwar entertainment fantasy. The picture was the highest grossing film of 1954 and the biggest commercial success of Curtiz’s career. Its vision of veterans converting wartime loyalty into theatrical prosperity helped define the polished, commercially optimistic American musical of the period.

    💡 💡 Intellectual Depth

    The film’s ideas are modest but more interesting than its confectionary surface suggests. Bob and Phil’s military bond becomes a business partnership, while General Waverly’s failing inn represents a displaced wartime hierarchy trying to survive in civilian life. The climactic Christmas show repairs both the inn and the characters’ damaged sense of belonging. Yet the film avoids serious conflict once its misunderstandings are resolved, and its nostalgia smooths over the social tensions it briefly raises. Its complexity is emotional and structural, not philosophical.

    7 / 10

    POUND + Truth & Soul Book Launch

    AFS · Film

    • Sun, Dec 13 · 3:00 PM

    Actors play pound animals in a claustrophobic Off-Off Broadway experiment.

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    Downey stages a single night in a city pound as a compressed theatrical laboratory. Eighteen dogs, a cat, and a penguin are played by human actors, with behavior and speech replacing makeup and costume. The device produces a deliberately artificial performance style, closer to an Off-Off Broadway experiment than conventional cinema. The film’s strongest formal choice is its confinement, which turns waiting, bickering, sexual aggression, and fear of execution into the material of the drama. Its weakness is equally clear. The staging can feel static, and the camera rarely transforms the theatrical premise into a fully cinematic language. Still, the ensemble’s sharply differentiated physical types, including the Boxer, Greyhound, and Mexican Hairless, give the film a durable comic architecture.

    ✨ Originality

    The premise remains genuinely strange. Human beings do not imitate dogs through costumes, but through social behavior, status games, bodily habits, and absurdly literal breed identities. That choice avoids sentimental animal storytelling and makes the pound an unstable miniature society. The parallel plot involving the Honky Killer and the inmates’ possible revolt expands the film beyond a waiting-room farce, though it also creates tonal clutter. The result is uneven but inventive, with “jailhouse humping” and funk-driven interruptions pushing the comedy toward unruly counterculture rather than polished satire.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    Pound belongs to the American underground tradition that used cheap, abrasive comedy to attack respectable social behavior. Its allegory of confinement, hierarchy, expendability, and institutional violence gives the animal shelter a clear political charge without reducing the film to a single topical message. The picture also marks the screen debut of five-year-old Robert Downey Jr. as Puppy, a fact of film history but not a substitute for artistic value. Its generally negative reception and later obscurity suggest that its abrasive form limited its immediate reach, while its survival in repertory screenings confirms its value as a singular artifact of early 1970s art cinema.

    💡 Intellectual Depth

    The film’s central idea is simple and productive. A society awaiting death reveals its rules more nakedly than a society pretending to be free. Breed becomes class, personality becomes destiny, and the pound’s bureaucratic machinery turns individual life into a disposal problem. Downey complicates that allegory with contradictory impulses, hope for a governor’s pardon, fantasies of escape, sexual rivalry, and the external threat of a serial killer. The film does not resolve these tensions into a coherent philosophy. Its thinking arrives through collision and performance, not argument. That disorder is part of its intelligence, but it also keeps Pound from reaching the sustained complexity of a masterpiece.

    Overall judgment:

    A strong, unruly experiment whose conceptual invention exceeds its cinematic control. It earns a 7 for converting a theatrical conceit into a sharp social allegory, even when the film’s pacing and stagebound execution work against it.

    Home Alone 2

    Paramount · Film

    • Mon, Dec 7 · 7:30 PM

    Kevin's New York traps show spatial logic, but the sequel's charm fades.

    Movie details.

    Home Alone 2: Lost in New York is a 1992 American English language comedy, directed by Chris Columbus, written and produced by John Hughes, with a running time of about 120 minutes.

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    Columbus stages the film with efficient visual clarity, especially in the hotel sequences and the abandoned townhouse climax. The traps have strong spatial logic, escalating from electrocution and falling tools to the repeated brick attacks, while Macaulay Culkin sustains the film’s rhythm through precise reaction shots and controlled physical comedy. The execution is polished, but the violence grows repetitive and punitive, limiting the film’s tonal range.

    ✨ Originality

    The New York setting adds scale, color, and a few fresh comic environments, including the Plaza Hotel and Duncan’s Toy Chest. The central structure, however, reproduces the first film almost scene for scene, with Kevin separated from his family, the same burglars returning, and a domestic fortress awaiting invasion. It is an accomplished variation, not an inventive sequel.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    The film helped establish the modern holiday sequel as a repeatable commercial form, preserving the original’s family comedy, child autonomy, seasonal spectacle, and elaborate slapstick. Its images, especially Kevin’s hotel luxury and the townhouse traps, remain central to the franchise’s identity. Its cultural importance rests more on durability and ritual viewing than on artistic advancement.

    💡 Intellectual Depth

    The film offers a simple but workable fantasy of independence. Kevin treats adult institutions, hotel staff, police, and family authority as obstacles, yet the closing reunion makes clear that freedom without attachment is incomplete. The elderly woman in Central Park supplies the strongest emotional counterpoint, giving Kevin someone else’s loneliness to recognize. Still, the film rarely develops these ideas beyond sentimental resolution, and its class assumptions remain largely unexamined.

    ROY FROM SPACE

    AFS · Film

    • Fri, Dec 11 · 9:00 PM
    • Sat, Dec 12 · 8:30 PM

    A scrappy Mexican animated space opera from 1979, charmingly rough.

    🎭 Artistic Merit. Roy From Space is a technically modest but formally determined animated feature. Its production history, made between 1979 and 1982, and its status as one of Mexico’s earliest feature length animated films give the work genuine industrial importance. The narrative follows Roy, Dr. Faz, and psychologist Elena to Mars, where they are captured by robots, escape with help from a princess, and join Prince Leo against the emperor Alome. That serial structure supplies momentum, but the film’s limited animation and homemade production methods constrain movement, staging, and dramatic timing. Its strongest achievement is persistence, not polish.

    ✨ Originality. The film’s central mixture of Mexican animation and space opera remains unusual, especially for 1983. Its plot openly draws on familiar pulp ingredients, the alien tyrant, royal resistance, robot guards, heroic astronaut, so invention lies less in story than in the decision to build an entire feature from those materials outside the dominant American studio system. The result is energetic but derivative, with episodic reversals replacing a more distinctive visual or narrative language.

    📚 Cultural Significance. Roy From Space matters as an artifact of Mexican animation history. It was only the third animated feature produced in Mexico and received a brief theatrical release in Mexico City before disappearing from circulation. Its recovery from a preserved 35 millimeter negative has restored evidence of a nearly forgotten national production tradition. That significance exceeds the film’s immediate artistic power, but it should not be dismissed as mere archival curiosity. The project demonstrates that Mexican filmmakers attempted feature animation despite weak infrastructure and limited commercial support.

    💡 Intellectual Depth. The film offers little sustained psychological or political complexity. Its conflict remains a clear contest between imperial conquest and collective resistance, while the characters function mainly as adventure roles. Elena’s presence as a psychologist suggests an opening toward questions of reason, fear, and human conduct in an alien environment, but the available plot structure does not develop those possibilities. The film’s deeper subject is practical and historical, the struggle to imagine a national science fiction cinema with scarce resources. That ambition gives it character, but not enough thematic density to place it above the competent range.
