    The Wailing

    Hyperreal · Film

    • Tue, Oct 27 · 7:30 PM

    A bumbling cop's murder case curdles into demonic possession and end-times dread.

    The supplied metadata is incorrect. The Wailing is a 2016 South Korean film, written and directed by Na Hong-jin, with a 156 minute runtime and Korean as its original language.

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    Na Hong-jin builds a demanding horror film from tonal collision. The opening murder investigation plays like bleak rural police comedy, then gives way to possession horror, ritual spectacle, and apocalyptic dread. Kwak Do-won’s performance as Jong-goo anchors the shifts. His incompetent policeman becomes a frantic father, and finally a man destroyed by the need to choose without reliable evidence. The film’s length is excessive in places, but its muddy landscapes, harsh daylight, grotesque makeup, and abrupt violence create a precise atmosphere of social collapse. The climactic parallel rituals, crosscut between competing forms of exorcism, are staged with exceptional control.

    ✨ Originality

    The film takes familiar materials, a suspicious stranger, a cursed village, a shaman, a possessed child, and an exorcism, then refuses to stabilize their meaning. Its central device is not simply concealment, but contradictory interpretation. The Japanese outsider may be demon, scapegoat, or both. The female stranger appears as protector and threat. The shaman’s performance may be spiritual practice, fraud, or opportunism. Even the final warnings become traps. This instability gives the film unusually high narrative entropy, though several comic interludes delay the horror rather than deepen it.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    The Wailing expands Korean horror beyond private terror into a conflict involving local superstition, institutional failure, Christian imagery, and historical suspicion toward Japan. The village’s fear is social before it becomes supernatural. Police incompetence, gossip, xenophobia, and religious commerce turn uncertainty into collective violence. Critics have recognized the film as a major Korean horror achievement, with particular attention to its mystery structure and engagement with postcolonial paranoia.

    💡 Intellectual Depth

    The film’s strongest idea is that evil thrives where judgment must be made without knowledge. Jong-goo repeatedly demands certainty, but every authority figure offers a compromised explanation. The result is not a puzzle with a clean solution, but a study of belief under pressure. Fathers, policemen, shamans, Christians, and villagers all convert fear into action, often worsening the danger. The final catastrophe does not prove one interpretation so much as expose the cost of choosing one too confidently. That moral and epistemic instability makes the film substantially more than an exercise in supernatural shock.

    Black Is Not a Genre presents: NOPE

    Hyperreal · Film

    • Wed, Oct 28 · 7:30 PM

    Peele's *Nope* uses scale, silence, and controlled revelation.

    🎭 🎭 Artistic Merit

    Jordan Peele’s Nope is a formally assured science fiction horror film built around scale, silence, and controlled revelation. The California ranch setting gives the film a stark visual field, while the night sequences use darkness as an active compositional force rather than mere concealment. Daniel Kaluuya plays OJ with restrained concentration, and Keke Palmer gives Emerald a volatile, comic energy that prevents the film from becoming solemn. The Gordy’s Home flashback, especially its suspended shoe and terrified chimpanzee, is one of the film’s strongest constructed sequences, though its connection to the central plot is deliberately indirect. The film’s 135 minute duration occasionally exposes its loose structure, but the imagery and sound design sustain its authority.

    ✨ ✨ Originality

    The film’s most inventive decision is to treat the alien presence as an animal with feeding patterns, territorial behavior, and visual limitations, rather than as a humanoid invader. Its shifting form turns the familiar flying saucer into a biological threat. Peele also links UFO spectacle to ranch labor, filmmaking, celebrity culture, and the history of Black participation in cinema. The film resists a clean explanatory mythology, but its themes sometimes arrive in parallel tracks rather than a perfectly integrated design. The result is unusual, unpredictable, and substantially less formulaic than its premise suggests.

    📚 📚 Cultural Significance

    Nope expands contemporary American genre cinema by placing a Black family at the center of a Western science fiction spectacle, with horses, open land, and inherited expertise treated as sources of knowledge rather than background decoration. The film’s opening reference to the first moving image of a Black rider establishes cinema as both an archive of exclusion and a field of possible recovery. Jupe’s attempt to turn catastrophe into a commercial attraction sharpens the film’s critique of entertainment culture, where danger becomes valuable once it can be packaged. Its contribution is strongest in the way it joins Black Western history to the machinery of blockbuster spectacle.

    💡 💡 Intellectual Depth

    The film examines the human appetite to look, record, possess, and monetize what should instead be approached with caution. OJ’s patient observation contrasts with Jupe’s performance driven exploitation and Antlers Holst’s destructive pursuit of the perfect image. These characters embody competing responses to spectacle, stewardship, commerce, and artistic ambition. The Gordy sequence complicates the central argument by showing how trauma can also become an industry asset. Nope does not resolve every symbolic connection, and its ideas are more suggestive than fully organized, but its best scenes convert abstraction into behavior. The film understands that the urge to witness can be both a survival skill and a form of predation.

Frederick Elmes’s saturated images and Angelo Badalamenti’s uneasy score give the film a firm visual and sonic identity; Nicolas Cage and Laura Dern commit fully to its unstable register. That inventiveness is real, though Lynch occasionally pushes the grotesque until it risks becoming a mannerism.

    💡 Intellectual Depth – Beneath the spectacle, the film tests whether love can survive a world shaped by violence, family abuse, and trauma. Lula’s memories give the road journey a psychological cost, while the Wizard of Oz references set dreams of escape against the film’s brutal reality. The ideas are sharper than the plot’s loose construction, which prefers feverish episodes to sustained moral inquiry.

    THE DEVILS

    AFS · Film

    • Sat, Oct 31 · 2:35 PM
    • Sat, Oct 31 · 8:15 PM
    • Sun, Nov 1 · 7:30 PM

    Ferocious, diseased spectacle of possession and execution, anchored by Reed's Grandier.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, Ken Russell stages The Devils with ferocious control of image and tempo, turning historical atrocity into a sequence of hard, ugly set pieces that rarely sit still. The white, skeletal set for Loudun, the mass possession scenes, and the final execution all show a director who understands how to make spectacle feel diseased rather than decorative. Oliver Reed’s Grandier gives the film a physical center, swaggering, bruised, and finally cornered, while Vanessa Redgrave’s Sister Jeanne makes hysteria feel organized, not random.

    ✨ Originality, this is one of the strangest major studio films of its era, and it still looks uncaged. Russell pushes sacred imagery, political satire, sexual repression, and operatic excess into the same frame, and the result is unstable in a way most period horrors never risk. The convent sequences, especially the “possession” material and the later public humiliation, refuse polite genre logic and keep shifting from grotesque comedy to terror to accusation.

    📚 Cultural Significance, the film remains a key case study in censorship, blasphemy panic, and the limits of British screen permissiveness. Its notoriety has lasted because it attacked institutions rather than merely offending taste, and because its production design and performance style gave that attack real cinematic force. The film’s reputation as one of Russell’s defining works is justified by how often later critics still return to it when discussing controversial art cinema.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, beneath the provocation is a clear argument about power. Grandier is destroyed not just by lust or clerical corruption, but by a machinery that turns private desire into public spectacle and uses religious language to cover political violence. The film is strongest when it links sexual panic, institutional cruelty, and mass gullibility, though its ideas are delivered with such racket and frenzy that subtlety is not its goal.

    AFS · Film

    • Sat, Oct 31 · 3:45 PM
    • Sun, Nov 1 · 3:00 PM

    A sly, deadpan comic essay using concrete's history to map American absurdity.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, John Wilson turns a ridiculous premise into a sturdy comic structure, using the search for concrete’s history as a way to move through sidewalks, monuments, city labor, and the low-grade absurdity of American public space. The film’s craft seems strongest in its patient accumulation of odd encounters and visual digressions, with the opening idea, a Hallmark-style pitch for a concrete documentary, giving the piece a sly formal engine rather than a mere gimmick.

    ✨ Originality, this is not a standard documentary, and it gains force from that refusal. The concept bends between satire, essay film, and city symphony, then keeps mutating as Wilson drifts from a commercial pitch exercise into questions about urbanism, memory, and the texture of ordinary life. That shape is uncommon enough to feel genuinely alive, and the comedy comes from structure, not just punch lines.

    📚 Cultural Significance, the film matters as a feature debut that extends Wilson’s TV practice into a longer form without sanding off its peculiar voice. Its subject, concrete, is a blunt material with huge civic reach, and the film uses that fact to think about New York, public life, and the anonymous architecture people move through every day. It may not alter documentary history, but it clearly belongs in the recent wave of essayistic nonfiction that treats the overlooked as a record of culture.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, beneath the comic premise, the film appears to build a serious argument about how cities are made, how value attaches to materials, and how human life leaves marks in supposedly inert things. The best reports on the film describe it moving from joke to reflection without breaking its comic rhythm, with concrete becoming a route into labor, mortality, and the gap between what is built and what is remembered. That is not empty cleverness, it is a coherent idea with enough range to support a full feature.

    Ray Gunn

    Paramount · Film

    • Sat, Oct 31 · 6:00 PM

    Brad Bird’s sci-fi noir boasts retro spaceships and Rockwell’s hardboiled comic rhythm.

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    Brad Bird’s Ray Gunn is a technically assured animated science fiction noir, built around the retrofuturist city of Metropia, where humans, aliens, flying cars, and prewar architectural styles share the same frame. The visual design appears to be the film’s chief achievement, while Sam Rockwell’s performance gives the private detective a hardboiled comic rhythm, and Michael Giacchino’s score reinforces the film’s classical noir machinery. The weakness is structural, the extended runtime and occasional story awkwardness blunt the momentum of an otherwise controlled production.

    ✨ Originality

    The film’s central combination, animated noir, science fiction mystery, and adult-oriented detective fiction, gives it a clear identity beyond standard family animation. Its strongest invention is the collision between old Hollywood genre codes and a future populated by humans and aliens. The premise remains familiar, however, and the detective plot reportedly depends on established noir conventions rather than radically reworking them.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    Ray Gunn matters chiefly as a major animated feature made for audiences beyond the usual family market, a direction Bird has defended as a legitimate use of animation. Its three-decade development also gives the film unusual industrial significance, since it represents a long-delayed attempt to bring adult animation, stylized genre filmmaking, and large-scale theatrical craft into the same project. Its lasting influence cannot yet be judged, because the film’s public release and broader reception remain recent or forthcoming.

    💡 Intellectual Depth

    The film’s most substantial idea concerns the protection and exploitation of art, expressed through the plot involving music superstar Venus Nova and the secret surrounding her. That premise links celebrity, ownership, performance, and private identity to the detective story. The material has genuine thematic potential, but the available evidence points to a film more interested in genre pleasure and design than sustained philosophical inquiry. Its complexity is strongest in the collision of artistic commerce with personal freedom, not in the mystery’s mechanics.

    CARRIE

    AFS · Film

    • Sat, Oct 31 · 7:00 PM

    De Palma's prom bloodbath turns adolescent humiliation into a ruinous spectacle.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, De Palma gives the material real formal punch, especially in the prom sequence, where the slow buildup, split focus, and sudden blood dump turn adolescent humiliation into a coordinated crash of image and sound. The final stretch, Carrie’s return home, her mother’s knife attack, and the house collapsing into fire and debris, shows a director who knows how to escalate a scene past shock into ruin. Piper Laurie’s performance adds hard-edged theatrical force, while Sissy Spacek keeps Carrie’s shy, watchful fragility believable enough to make the violence land.

    ✨ Originality, The film avoids generic teen-horror routine by binding telekinesis to embarrassment, religion, and sexual panic, then making the prom feel less like a set piece than a social execution. The ending, with Sue’s nightmare of Carrie’s hand bursting from the grave, became one of horror’s most copied jolts because it is simple, brutal, and perfectly timed.

    📚 Cultural Significance, Carrie became a landmark horror title and one of the defining films of 1976, widely praised on release and still treated as a key Stephen King adaptation. Its influence is visible in later school-horror, revenge, and prom-night nightmare stories, and its image of the blood-soaked girl at the center of a crowd has become part of the genre’s visual vocabulary.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, The film’s ideas are plain but not thin, it studies cruelty as a group act, shame as a trigger, and faith as a weapon when filtered through Carrie’s mother. What gives it weight is the clash between private humiliation and public spectacle, a social mechanism made literal when the prank at the prom turns a teenage outcast into an agent of destruction.

    BEGOTTEN

    AFS · Film

    • Sat, Oct 31 · 9:30 PM

    Wordless creation myth in grainy black-and-white, a relentless assault on film grammar.

    ★ Rating: [8/10]

    🎭 Artistic Merit, Merhige makes the film on a shoestring into an act of visual sabotage, with grainy black and white imagery, no dialogue, and sound built from wind, breathing, water, and animal noise. The result is crude in the best sense, a rigorously controlled assault on conventional film grammar, though its severity can feel willfully punishing rather than fully controlled.

    ✨ Originality, This is the film’s strongest asset. Its creation myth structure, the death, dismemberment, and rebirth imagery, and the refusal of normal narrative make it hard to mistake for anything else. The distorted monochrome look, described by critics as closer to silent-era expressionism and underground moving-image art than horror convention, gives it a damaged, antique texture that still feels alien.

    📚 Cultural Significance, Begotten has remained a reference point for extreme experimental horror, especially for filmmakers interested in mixing religious allegory, body horror, and formal deprivation. Its reputation is larger than its audience, but that is part of its role, it proved that American independent cinema could push past story and character into pure image stress.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, The film is thin on explanation and heavy on symbolic aggression, but that does not make it empty. Its cycle of destruction, violation, and rebirth points toward a bleak cosmology about nature, sacrifice, and the collapse of divine authority, with enough ambiguity to invite interpretation without collapsing into it.

    Little Miss Sunshine

    Paramount · Film

    • Sun, Nov 1 · 12:30 PM

    A broken family's yellow van ride to a child's pageant, darkly funny.

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    Jonathan Dayton and Valerie Faris turn a modest road comedy into a tightly controlled ensemble piece. The yellow Volkswagen van becomes both setting and pressure chamber, forcing the family into visual and emotional proximity. The film’s strongest construction arrives at the pageant, where Olive’s grotesque dance exposes the adults’ failures more clearly than any speech could. Alan Arkin, Toni Collette, Steve Carell, Greg Kinnear, Paul Dano, and Abigail Breslin sustain distinct comic rhythms without collapsing into caricature. The film is polished, but its visual language remains functional rather than formally daring.

    ✨ Originality

    Michael Arndt’s screenplay refreshes familiar dysfunctional family and road movie conventions through sharply opposed belief systems. Richard’s sales rhetoric, Dwayne’s vow of silence, Frank’s academic humiliation, and Grandpa’s reckless appetite all create competing definitions of success. The film’s final act risks sentimental resolution, but the pageant’s deliberately bad choreography and the family’s collective decision to join Olive keep the conclusion from becoming ordinary uplift.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    Released in 2006, the film became a major independent success, earning four Academy Award nominations and wins for Original Screenplay and Supporting Actor. It helped establish the commercially viable family dramedy as a space for adult depression, failure, sexuality, and economic anxiety, while Abigail Breslin’s nomination marked the performance as a central part of its public impact. Its cultural reach is substantial, though its influence is more industrial and popular than formally revolutionary.

    💡 Intellectual Depth

    The film’s central argument is precise. A culture that treats winning as proof of personal worth damages both children and adults. Richard sells confidence while privately collapsing, Dwayne discovers that discipline cannot control reality, and Frank’s intellectual authority offers no protection from humiliation. Olive’s innocence is not presented as pure wisdom, but her lack of investment in the family’s ranking system allows the others to imagine solidarity. The film does not abolish failure. It changes the family’s response to it, which is its most durable insight.

    Public Curator-Led Tour: The Living Temple: The World of Moki Cherry & HOST: Laura Lit

    Jones Center · Visual Arts

    • Sun, Nov 1 · 2:00 PM

    Domestic scale meets theatrical display in Moki Cherry's Living Temple.

    🎭 Formal Qualities

    The exhibition makes its strongest case through range and arrangement. Textiles, costumes, ceramics, posters, video, sound, and archival material occupy the Jones Center as parts of one expanded environment, rather than as isolated objects. Cherry’s technique joins domestic scale with theatrical display, using fabric, color, and improvised structures to blur the line between a room, a stage, and a social gathering. The inclusion of performance documentation and workshop material gives the installation a useful sense of movement, though the abundance of media risks turning the exhibition into an inventory of activities rather than a tightly edited argument.

    ✨ Originality

    Cherry’s refusal to separate painting, design, performance, education, and daily life remains the exhibition’s central achievement. Her work proposes the home and the communal workshop as artistic infrastructure, not secondary settings for finished objects. That approach now belongs to a familiar strain of socially engaged and interdisciplinary practice, but Cherry’s combination of textiles, sound, costumes, and collective action predates much of today’s institutional vocabulary. The exhibition earns its score by showing this as a lived system, not merely as a set of progressive themes.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    The Jones Center presentation is significant because it introduces Austin audiences to a major Swedish artist whose practice has long sat outside standard accounts of postwar art. Its emphasis on collaboration, shared learning, and the dissolution of boundaries between art and ordinary life directly addresses current debates about authorship, care, domestic labor, and community space. The pairing with HOST, Laura Lit’s drawings and sculptures, strengthens the institutional frame, although the two exhibitions appear more adjacent than genuinely integrated.

    💡 Historical Context

    Moki Cherry, born in 1943 and deceased in 2009, worked across visual art, design, performance, and social practice. The exhibition places her in a lineage that includes experimental theater, textile-based abstraction, feminist domestic critique, and communal avant-garde projects. Its historical value lies in correcting the old hierarchy that treated painting and sculpture as serious art while relegating costume, decoration, workshops, and household space to supporting roles. The presentation is strong because it makes Cherry’s entire artistic ecology visible, though its retrospective scale also exposes how much critical reassessment remains to be done.

    THE CONJURING

    Hyperreal · Film

    • Fri, Oct 30 · 7:30 PM

    Wan's patient direction makes every shadow and creak a promise of dread.

    🎭 🎭 Artistic Merit

    James Wan directs with disciplined control of space, sound, and delayed revelation. The Perron farmhouse becomes a readable visual system, with long corridors, dark stairwells, and rooms that conceal threats just outside the frame. The hide and clap sequence, the wardrobe apparition, and the basement attack show a director who understands that anticipation often works better than spectacle. Vera Farmiga and Patrick Wilson give the Warrens warmth and procedural focus, while Lili Taylor makes Carolyn’s breakdown physically legible. The film’s weakness is its polished dependence on familiar supernatural machinery, including abrupt shocks, possession effects, and explanatory flashbacks. Still, its execution is consistently stronger than its material. The film runs 112 minutes and was directed by James Wan in 2013.

    ✨ ✨ Originality

    The film does not reinvent the haunted house story. Its originality lies in arrangement rather than premise. It combines the domestic scale of a family drama with the investigative structure of a case file, then gives the Warrens a stable marriage that functions as the story’s emotional counterweight. The camera repeatedly withholds information instead of displaying the monster, especially during the clap game and the shadowed bedroom searches. The Bathsheba mythology is conventional and overexplained, and the claim of supernatural authenticity adds little artistic complexity. This is an intelligent refinement of genre formulas, not a radical revision of them.

    📚 📚 Cultural Significance

    The film helped restore commercial prestige to studio supernatural horror, earning more than 319 million dollars worldwide against a 20 million dollar budget and generating an extensive franchise. Its influence is visible in the later industry preference for interconnected demonologies, investigator protagonists, and supposedly documented cases. That success also exposes a limitation. The film turns the Warrens’ controversial paranormal worldview into reassuring entertainment, replacing uncertainty with a clean conflict between demonic evil and Christian protection. Its contribution is substantial within popular horror, though more industrial than formally revolutionary.

    💡 💡 Intellectual Depth

    The strongest idea is not the existence of ghosts, but the corruption of parental protection. Bathsheba’s violence targets mothers, turning care for children into a weapon against them. Carolyn’s possession therefore threatens both bodily autonomy and the moral identity of the family. The film also presents faith as a practical social bond, embodied in the Warrens’ marriage and the Perrons’ efforts to remain together. These ideas give the scares emotional stakes, but the screenplay resolves them too neatly. Historical trauma, mental illness, and religious certainty receive little scrutiny once the demon is identified. The result is thematically coherent and psychologically accessible, but not especially deep.

Cut and sanded metal, reflective vinyl, ink glazes, acrylic, linen, and wood panel create surfaces that alternate between abrasion, opacity, and glare. The process is materially convincing, especially in Shapeshifter, where reflective vinyl makes the image change with the viewer’s position. The available descriptions establish a strong interest in rupture and replacement, but provide too little visual documentation to judge the exhibition’s overall composition, installation, or consistency. The technique is more compelling than the stated metaphor.

    ✨ Originality

    The exhibition’s central idea, identity as something capable of holding several states at once, is familiar. Boley’s stronger contribution lies in forcing that idea through resistant materials. The cut metal and reflective surfaces do not merely illustrate transformation, they make the viewer experience instability through changing reflections and interrupted planes. Still, the conceptual language of tension, change, and selfhood remains broad. The work earns its score through material decisions, not through a radically new premise.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    As Boley’s first solo exhibition, shapeshifting introduces an Austin painter working outside the clean separation between painting and object. Its concern with mutable identity fits contemporary art’s continuing attention to self construction, visibility, and unstable categories. The gallery’s local and international program gives the show a useful institutional setting, but the available evidence does not support claims of major critical impact or a decisive intervention in current discourse. Its significance is presently regional and emergent.

    💡 Historical Context

    Boley extends a lineage of painting that treats the picture surface as a physical structure rather than a transparent window. The combination of metal, sanding, reflective material, and layered paint recalls postwar material abstraction, shaped painting, and later practices that make the viewer’s body part of the image. Her earlier appearance in the group exhibition between us places this solo show at an early career turning point, but there is not enough documented work to map a substantial artistic trajectory. shapeshifting is a strong debut, with real formal promise and an explanatory framework that needs greater specificity.
