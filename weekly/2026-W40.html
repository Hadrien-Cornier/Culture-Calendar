    MY NEIGHBOR TOTORO

    AFS · Film

    • Sat, Oct 3 · 6:45 PM
    • Sun, Oct 4 · 3:15 PM

    Umbrella scene proves Miyazaki's craft: silence and gesture carry more weight than sentiment.

    🎭 Artistic Merit Hayao Miyazaki turns a slight story into a film of exacting control. The animation has uncommon confidence in movement, the girls’ walk through the countryside, the rustle of grass, the slow reveal of Totoro at the bus stop, and the film trusts silence where lesser work would force sentiment. The umbrella scene is the clearest proof, a rain-soaked exchange built from timing, gesture, and sound, with Totoro’s delighted reaction carrying more weight than dialogue ever could. The Catbus arrival pushes the design into something both silly and impeccably precise, a machine-like fantasy creature that still feels part of the film’s everyday world.

    ✨ Originality The film’s innovation lies in how little it explains. Totoro is not reduced to allegory, and the movie refuses the usual fantasy habit of overloading the screen with rules, quests, or threats. Instead it treats childhood perception as a credible mode of experience, where a tree’s growth, a shadow in the dark, or a creature waiting in the rain can feel equally real. That restraint makes the film hard to predict and hard to pigeonhole.

    📚 Cultural Significance Its cultural reach is enormous, not because it chases spectacle, but because it helped define Studio Ghibli’s identity and became one of the most recognizable images in world animation. The umbrella scene, the Catbus, and Totoro himself have become part of global visual culture, which is rare for a film so quiet and domestic in scale. It also broadened the idea that animated films could live comfortably in ordinary space, with family, weather, and rural routine as the main event.

    💡 Intellectual Depth The film’s ideas are simple on the surface, but not shallow. It treats illness, uncertainty, sibling responsibility, and the fragility of childhood security without speeches or melodrama, letting them sit inside the girls’ daily life. The mother’s absence gives the film a low hum of anxiety, and the fantasy does not erase that worry, it gives the children a way to endure it. That mix of comfort and unease gives the movie real depth, even when nothing “important” is happening in plot terms.

    Somos Animales Poéticos

    Dougherty Arts Center · Visual Arts

    • Sat, Oct 3 · 10:00 AM

    Photographs disrupt portraiture with archival animals, textiles, and literary collages.

    🎭 Formal Qualities

    Velásquez treats photography as a layered construction rather than a transparent record. The exhibition combines photographs, archival imagery, literary fragments, collage, staged reenactment, and textile references. Animal images from a 1950s informational anthology are placed over human faces, while floral patterns and tactile surfaces disrupt the authority of the photographic portrait. The strongest formal decision is the refusal of fixed hierarchy, with image, text, fabric, and background functioning as parts of one woven field. The installation appears visually controlled, though the fluid presentation and absence of titles may reduce individual works to variations within a single register.

    ✨ Originality

    The exhibition is strongest when it makes familiar photographic materials behave strangely. Velásquez does not simply add collage to documentary photography, she uses archival intervention to question who gets represented, classified, and made legible. The animal-covered portraits turn identity into an unstable exchange between human subject and inherited image. This is a convincing departure from conventional documentary practice, although its language of textiles, memory, hybridity, and personal mythology belongs to an established field of contemporary photographic collage.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    Velásquez connects Colombian identity, motherhood, migration, and life in Austin to wider debates about colonial portraiture and the limits of documentary photography. Her use of literature and feminisms from the Global South gives the exhibition a clear critical position, while the Dougherty Arts Center places that position within a public, community-oriented institution rather than an exclusively specialist photography venue. The work matters most when it turns cultural displacement into a problem of looking, not merely a biographical subject.

    💡 Historical Context

    The exhibition belongs to a contemporary lineage that merges photography with collage, archival recovery, artists’ books, and feminist revision of documentary traditions. Velásquez’s background between Austin and Bogotá, her MFA training through Bard College’s International Center of Photography program, and her ongoing movement between photographic genres place the series within a transnational photographic practice. Somos Animales Poéticos also extends the artist’s established interest in weaving, translation, rupture, and repair, while marking a mature consolidation of those concerns in the form of a book and exhibition.

    THE HISTORY OF CONCRETE

    AFS · Film

    • Sat, Oct 3 · 12:30 PM
    • Sat, Oct 3 · 7:15 PM
    • Sat, Oct 3 · 9:45 PM
    • Sun, Oct 4 · 8:00 PM

    John Wilson turns concrete into a sly, digressive comic study of American space.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, John Wilson turns a ridiculous premise into a sturdy comic structure, using the search for concrete’s history as a way to move through sidewalks, monuments, city labor, and the low-grade absurdity of American public space. The film’s craft seems strongest in its patient accumulation of odd encounters and visual digressions, with the opening idea, a Hallmark-style pitch for a concrete documentary, giving the piece a sly formal engine rather than a mere gimmick.

    ✨ Originality, this is not a standard documentary, and it gains force from that refusal. The concept bends between satire, essay film, and city symphony, then keeps mutating as Wilson drifts from a commercial pitch exercise into questions about urbanism, memory, and the texture of ordinary life. That shape is uncommon enough to feel genuinely alive, and the comedy comes from structure, not just punch lines.

    📚 Cultural Significance, the film matters as a feature debut that extends Wilson’s TV practice into a longer form without sanding off its peculiar voice. Its subject, concrete, is a blunt material with huge civic reach, and the film uses that fact to think about New York, public life, and the anonymous architecture people move through every day. It may not alter documentary history, but it clearly belongs in the recent wave of essayistic nonfiction that treats the overlooked as a record of culture.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, beneath the comic premise, the film appears to build a serious argument about how cities are made, how value attaches to materials, and how human life leaves marks in supposedly inert things. The best reports on the film describe it moving from joke to reflection without breaking its comic rhythm, with concrete becoming a route into labor, mortality, and the gap between what is built and what is remembered. That is not empty cleverness, it is a coherent idea with enough range to support a full feature.

    AIA Austin LGBTQIA+ Alliance presents URINAL

    AFS · Film

    • Sat, Oct 3 · 3:30 PM

    Queer artists debate history through CCTV, cruising and collage.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, John Greyson makes a lean, combative debut feature out of public sex, police surveillance, and queer history, and the form matches the argument. The mix of narrative invention, documentary material, and video, computer paintbox effects gives the film a scrappy but deliberate look, more collage than conventional drama, and that suits a movie built around entrapment, performance, and public shame. Its best scenes, the summoned dead artists arguing over washroom policing, turn talk into theater without softening the politics.

    ✨ Originality, this is not a polite issue film. Greyson gives the investigation to Frida Kahlo, Sergei Eisenstein, Yukio Mishima, Langston Hughes, and Dorian Gray, which is a strange, witty, and genuinely risky conceit. The result feels unstable in a productive way, part satire, part essay, part queer séance, and it avoids the usual social problem movie beats. The dead do not appear as decorative icons, they are pressed into service as investigators, infiltrators, and moral irritants.

    📚 Cultural Significance, the film matters as an early Canadian response to police crackdowns on gay sex venues and washroom entrapment, and it translates a specific legal panic into art with political force. Its festival life, including Berlin’s Teddy Award recognition, marks it as part of the emergence of queer essay cinema, and its archive value is strong because it records a period when surveillance and public naming could wreck lives. The film’s directness about policing and suicide gives it more than historical interest.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, Greyson is thinking about how cities regulate bodies, how public morality gets enforced, and how queer desire survives under exposure. The film’s central idea, that dead artists can be conscripted to investigate living repression, is playful on the surface but sharp underneath, because it asks who gets to speak for sexual dissent across history. It is busiest where it should be, in the clash between iconography, bureaucracy, and desire, and that gives it real intellectual bite.

    THE ROCKY HORROR PICTURE SHOW

    AFS · Film

    • Sat, Oct 3 · 9:00 PM
    • Sun, Oct 4 · 5:30 PM

    Tim Curry's theatrical menace and campy wit define this cult midnight

    🎭 Artistic Merit, Jim Sharman’s film is rough in places, but its control of tone is stronger than its reputation suggests. Tim Curry’s Frank-N-Furter dominates every scene he enters, especially the introduction in the laboratory and the “Sweet Transvestite” number, where the film finds its sharpest blend of theatricality, sexual threat, and comic timing. The production leans hard on set design, costume, and music rather than visual polish, yet that stripped, studio-bound artificiality becomes part of the style.

    ✨ Originality, The film pushes camp, glam, horror, and rock musical into one unstable mix, and it keeps changing registers before the viewer can settle in. Its gender play, audience-addressing confidence, and villain-as-showman structure were far from routine in 1975. Even when the jokes are broad and the plotting thin, the film’s sheer willingness to be vulgar, self-aware, and strange gives it high volatility.

    📚 Cultural Significance, Its initial critical failure matters less than the long afterlife it created. The midnight-movie phenomenon, audience call-backs, costume culture, and shadow casts turned it into a participatory ritual rather than a passive screening. That shift, plus its embrace by queer audiences and outsiders, made it a durable object in film culture, not just a period curiosity.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, The film is not philosophically dense, but it is smarter than a simple camp joke. It treats identity as performance, desire as unstable, and normality as brittle, then stages those ideas through bodies, clothes, and songs instead of dialogue. The real argument is in the behavior of the characters, the seductive confidence of Frank-N-Furter, and the way the “ordinary” couple is pulled into a world where social rules stop holding.
