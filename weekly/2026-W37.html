    GANDAHAR

    AFS · Film

    • Mon, Sep 7 · 2:00 PM
    • Thu, Sep 10 · 8:00 PM

    Surreal French animation where pastel aliens question humanity and technology.

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    Gandahar is a sharpened piece of French animation, with Laloux’s clean, elastic linework and pastel alien landscapes doing a lot of heavy lifting. The movie’s most striking craft choice is its refusal to imitate live action realism, it trusts abstraction, color, and odd anatomy to carry mood and worldbuilding, which keeps even the exposition scenes visually active. The animation is not as fluid or detailed as the best feature work of its era, but the design sense is exact, and the film’s birdlike cities, mutated bodies, and drifting horizons give it a consistent visual identity.

    ✨ Originality

    Its premise, a society attacked through time by a machine that turns people into stone, is not routine fantasy material, and the film keeps refusing generic heroics. Laloux and his collaborators build a world where political order, biology, and science fiction paranoia bleed into one another, and the result is stranger than a standard quest narrative. Specific images, like the petrified victims and the soft, almost toy-like surfaces of the landscape, make the film feel less like recycled genre and more like an eccentric fable with its own physics.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    Laloux matters because he helped prove that adult animation could carry speculative ideas without talking down to the viewer, and Gandahar sits squarely in that tradition. It is not a landmark on the scale of Fantastic Planet, but it extends the same European taste for political allegory, strange ecologies, and nonstandard visual logic. Its importance is cumulative, not explosive, yet it still contributes a distinct example of French animated science fiction that values ideas over spectacle.

    💡 Intellectual Depth

    The film’s real subject is not simply invasion, but the fragility of identity under technological control. Its time-travel frame, bodily mutation, and social fragility suggest a civilization that mistakes stability for permanence, then discovers how quickly a system can be hollowed out. The story also treats memory and transformation as political problems, not just science-fiction devices, which gives the film more weight than its streamlined running time might suggest. It is not philosophically dense in a literary sense, but it does more than decorate a chase plot with odd visuals.

    AMERICAN DOCTOR

    AFS · Film

    • Mon, Sep 7 · 2:30 PM
    • Tue, Sep 8 · 4:30 PM

    A lean, urgent war documentary on Gaza hospital labor, not abstract outrage.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, Poh Si Teng turns a war documentary into something lean, urgent, and controlled, with the camera staying close to the labor of medicine rather than drifting into abstract outrage. The strongest passages, by the accounts available, are the Gaza hospital scenes, where operating-room chaos, triage decisions, and exhausted staff carry the film’s force without overstatement. Minor first-feature strain is visible in some reviews, especially around occasional intrusive music, but the overall execution sounds disciplined and immediate.

    ✨ Originality, the film’s core device, three American doctors of Palestinian, Jewish, and Zoroastrian backgrounds moving through the same catastrophe, gives it built-in tension that avoids the usual single-hero documentary shape. Its second half, which reportedly follows the doctors back to Washington, sharpens the structure by shifting from emergency care to testimony, and that change of register keeps the film from becoming a one-note crisis chronicle. It earns points for making identity, medicine, and public witness collide without smoothing the contradictions.

    📚 Cultural Significance, this is plainly a document of Gaza’s medical collapse and of the politics around who gets believed. Its value also lies in the cast of witnesses, especially the juxtaposition of a Palestinian-American ER doctor, a Jewish orthopedic surgeon, and a Zoroastrian trauma surgeon, which broadens the film beyond familiar binary framing. The film seems built to travel, to theaters, festivals, and civic debate, and that gives it real historical weight.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, the film appears most intelligent when it asks who is permitted moral authority, who gets to be angry, and why some deaths become public facts while others are administratively blurred. That line of inquiry, paired with the doctors’ movement from hospital wards to Congress, gives the documentary a clear argument about evidence, empathy, and political language. It sounds less interested in easy catharsis than in the ugly gap between what is seen, what is said, and what institutions refuse to absorb.

    MYSTERIOUS SKIN

    AFS · Film

    • Mon, Sep 7 · 5:00 PM

    Gregg Araki’s unflinching film turns childhood trauma into a quiet, devastating alien abduction.

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    Gregg Araki’s film is unusually controlled for a director known for aggression and style, and its best scenes are built with brutal patience. The two parallel childhood tracks, one rendered as dreamy dissociation and the other as sexual menace, give the film real formal grip, while the performances, especially Brady Corbet and Max Roeg, keep the material from collapsing into thesis. The adult material is less emotionally supple than the childhood passages, but the film’s restraint in its final movement, and its refusal to tidy trauma into catharsis, show serious craft.

    ✨ Originality

    Its central move, splitting a child abuse narrative into separate subjective realities, is the film’s sharpest invention, and it keeps the story from turning into standard recovery drama. Araki avoids the usual grim social-problem template by filtering memory through texture, fantasy, and repression, so even familiar scenes, a swimming pool, a suburban bedroom, a strange encounter in the dark, arrive with unstable meaning. The structure is not gimmick; it is the film’s argument.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    The film matters because it brought queer sensibility, trauma, and suburban American rot into the same frame without sanding off the discomfort. It sits in a crucial line of early 2000s independent cinema that treated taboo material as formal subject matter, not just scandal bait. Its impact is strongest as a model of how abuse narratives can be told with cinematic intelligence rather than procedural solemnity.

    💡 Intellectual Depth

    The film is interested in how memory protects itself, how desire and violation can coexist in one psyche, and how a child can survive by inventing alternate meanings for what happens to him. That gives it more than shock value, because it asks what language, fantasy, and self-mythology can do when direct comprehension is unbearable. Its ideas are clear, but not simple, and the split-view structure gives those ideas real dramatic force.

    THIS IS NOT A FILM

    AFS · Film

    • Tue, Sep 8 · 7:30 PM
    • Sat, Sep 12 · 2:00 PM

    Panahi turns house arrest into a taut map of artistic confinement.

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    This is a low-budget work turned into style, not a limitation disguised as a virtue. Panahi builds tension from confinement, using the apartment, the staircase, the elevator ride, and the taped-out floor plan as a shrinking map of artistic and political imprisonment. The long takes and plain, unshowy framing force attention onto timing, silence, and the director’s own halting performance as he reads, speaks, and stops short of making the banned film. The result is disciplined, exact, and sharply controlled, with formal restraint doing real dramatic work.

    ✨ Originality

    The film’s central wager, making a film about a film that cannot legally be made, is bold and structurally alert. What gives it force is the way it turns ordinary domestic actions, phone calls, an apartment door, a ride in the elevator, into evidence of censorship’s daily mechanics. The inclusion of a young man with a phone camera, and later the move onto the building’s elevator and communal spaces, shifts the film from private testimony into a live demonstration of image-making under pressure.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    Its importance is inseparable from its conditions of production. As a work made under restriction, it registers a specific political reality in Iran while also speaking to the larger problem of who gets to image a society and who gets silenced. The film became widely discussed because it was not only a statement about censorship, but a practical act of defiance against it.

    💡 Intellectual Depth

    The film is strongest when it treats censorship not as an abstract theme, but as a daily distortion of thought, labor, and self-definition. Panahi’s taped floor plan, his rehearsal of scenes that will never be shot, and his anxious, rueful exchanges about what counts as a film all ask the same question, what survives when an artist is blocked from practice. It is a serious, unsentimental inquiry into authorship, freedom, and the gap between intention and action, with enough self-awareness to avoid turning suffering into easy symbolism.

    HIS HOUSE presented by Parallel Lens

    Hyperreal · Film

    • Tue, Sep 8 · 7:30 PM

    Refugee horror in cramped rooms where grief and blame become ghosts.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, Remi Weekes builds his horror out of cramped rooms, damp plaster, and the brittle silence between Bol and Rial, and that material discipline gives the film real force. The performances by Ṣọpẹ Dìrísù and Wunmi Mosaku carry the weight of grief and blame without turning theatrical, and the house itself becomes a practical pressure chamber rather than a generic scare machine. The craft is strongest when the film keeps its ghosts tied to concrete damage, a missing child, a failed journey, a home that never stops feeling temporary.

    ✨ Originality, The film avoids the usual haunted house formula by rooting terror in asylum, displacement, and survivor guilt, then turning the home into a legal and emotional trap. It is not subtle, but it is inventive in the way it fuses refugee realism with supernatural intrusion, and it keeps changing the terms of its own fear. That combination gives it a harder edge than standard genre work.

    📚 Cultural Significance, Its central subject, a South Sudanese couple trying to survive English bureaucracy after war and flight, gives the film clear relevance beyond genre audiences. The movie stands out as a horror work that treats migration as lived trauma rather than background issue, which gives it a distinct place in recent popular horror. Its value lies less in broad social commentary than in how directly it forces genre form to carry that burden.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, Beneath the shocks, the film is about memory as an occupier, marriage under strain, and the moral damage caused by survival itself. The strongest idea is simple and unsparing, a house can be safe only on paper, while the mind keeps rebuilding the disaster inside it. The screenplay does not always trust silence enough, but it does press its central question with enough clarity to matter, what does home mean when the past has followed you across the water.

    Black Is Not a Genre presents SIGN O' THE TIMES

    Hyperreal · Film

    • Wed, Sep 9 · 7:30 PM

    Prince's 1987 concert film, a theatrical funk spectacle with Sheila E. driving.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, the work has the force of a sharpened concert film, not a routine document of a tour. The performance footage centers Prince in top form, with Sheila E. driving the band and Sheena Easton adding a clean, theatrical jolt to the set, while the staged interludes keep the film from becoming a flat string of songs. The result is controlled, fast, and visually assertive, with enough formal play to feel planned rather than merely captured.

    ✨ Originality, it stands apart from standard live-music cinema by mixing performance with narrative fragments, including the Cat Glover cat and mouse thread that links numbers instead of simply introducing applause breaks. That structure gives the film a strange, highly specific rhythm, and Prince’s direction refuses the safe, broadcast-style grammar that usually weakens concert films.

    📚 Cultural Significance, the film matters because it presents Prince not just as a performer but as a total author of his image, sound, and stage world. Its continued critical afterlife, including strong contemporary reassessment and an 80 percent Rotten Tomatoes score, shows that it remains a durable reference point for concert filmmaking. In the context of Prince’s 1987 album, it also preserves a key moment when his pop vision, funk discipline, and theatrical instinct were working at full pressure.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, the film is not philosophically dense in a literary sense, but it does think clearly about performance, identity, and control. The contrast between live eruption and scripted vignettes suggests an artist testing how much personality can be carried by pure stage presence and how much needs framing, costume, and comic invention. That gives the work a real idea behind the spectacle, the self as an engineered event, rather than a loose parade of hits.

    REPO MAN

    AFS · Film

    • Wed, Sep 9 · 8:30 PM
    • Sat, Sep 12 · 6:45 PM

    Ugly LA repossession crews, punk rock, and a glowing Chevy Malibu collide.

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    Repo Man is a rough, fast, and deliberately unpolished piece of filmmaking, but that roughness is part of its control. Alex Cox keeps the 85 minutes moving with ugly storefronts, deadpan performances, and a Los Angeles that looks scraped raw, not glamorized. The pacing is clipped and impatient, and the film’s best scenes, Otto’s dead-end job, the car repossession routines, the sudden appearance of the glowing Malibu trunk, turn absurdity into a working rhythm rather than a gimmick.

    ✨ Originality

    This is the film’s strongest area. It refuses the usual punk movie template of rebellion as lifestyle branding and instead builds a logic of random collisions, corporate absurdism, alien conspiracy, and street-level labor. The jokes land because they are so specific, the pay phone philosophy, the alien car, the bureaucratic violence of repo work, the casual way the film jumps from satire to sci-fi without warning. It is unpredictable without feeling lazy, and that gives it real charge.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    The movie became a defining cult object of 1980s American indie cinema, in part because it captured a post-punk Los Angeles of cheap anger, scavenged identities, and economic drift. Its mix of genre parody and subcultural texture helped expand what a low-budget American movie could do, especially outside studio polish. The film’s influence is less about imitation than permission, it showed later filmmakers that speed, noise, and conceptual mismatch could be a style.

    💡 Intellectual Depth

    Its ideas are smarter than its swagger. Beneath the gags, Repo Man treats work as extraction, institutions as jokes, and America as a place where spiritual emptiness and consumer debris sit side by side. The film never turns these ideas into speeches, which is part of its intelligence, it lets the repo job, the blank faces, and the bizarre tailfin bureaucracy do the talking. The result is not subtle in the manner of high art, but it is conceptually agile and unusually alert to the absurd mechanics of modern life.

    Crayon Shin-chan: The Storm Called: The Adult Empire Strikes Back

    Hyperreal · Film

    • Thu, Sep 10 · 7:30 PM

    🎭 Artistic Merit. Keiichi Hara turns a children’s franchise film into a tightly controlled piece of animation cinema. The direction uses the theme park’s stiff retro design, the adults’ blank behavior, and the film’s shift from comic chaos to family panic to build real pressure, and the climax in the Expo’s sterile show spaces gives the movie a sharp visual logic. The craft is stronger than the premise suggests, with clear staging, disciplined pacing, and a final stretch that lands because it understands the emotional weight of ordinary domestic life.

    ✨ Originality. The central idea, adults willingly surrendering the present for a manufactured past, is not subtle, but the film pushes it through an anime comedy engine with unusual confidence. Its 20th-century nostalgia machine, with chintzy showmanship and fake memory, gives the story a specific, concrete absurdity instead of a generic “lost childhood” setup. The result is less a routine adventure than a satire of comfort, conformity, and emotional retreat.

    📚 Cultural Significance. The film stands out as one of the most respected entries in the Crayon Shin-chan line and helped raise Keiichi Hara’s profile as a director. It matters because it shows how mainstream animated entertainment can carry adult concerns without abandoning slapstick or family appeal, and because its nostalgia critique still fits a culture saturated with retro branding and curated memory.

    💡 Intellectual Depth. The movie’s best idea is simple and pointed, adults are not just nostalgic, they are vulnerable to the fantasy of escaping responsibility altogether. That gives the comedy a harder edge, especially in the way the parents’ regression strips away authority and leaves children to manage a world built by childish escapism. It is not a theory-heavy film, but it is unusually alert to how memory, labor, and regret can be packaged into entertainment, and that gives it more bite than most animated comedies.

    THE HOLE

    AFS · Film

    • Fri, Sep 11 · 7:00 PM

    Tsai Ming-liang’s contagion-era musical: rain, isolation, and abrupt cabaret.

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    Tsai Ming-liang’s The Hole is a lean, disciplined piece of formal control, built around a ruined apartment block, a leaking ceiling, and two bodies isolated by contagion and routine. The musical outbursts, especially the abrupt Kay Huang numbers, fracture the film’s grim stillness and keep the framing from settling into mere austerity; the contrast between drab interiors and those stylized performances is the film’s sharpest formal move. Its craft is strongest in duration, spacing, and the patience with which it lets small actions, a drip, a crawl, a glance through a floor opening, take on real weight.

    ✨ Originality

    The premise is simple, but the execution is unusual enough to feel unmistakably Tsai. Rather than treating the post-apocalyptic setting as spectacle, the film turns a water leak between apartments into a physical and emotional system, using vertical space, isolation, and repeated domestic gestures to generate unease and absurdity. The blend of social decay, deadpan comedy, and sudden musical fantasy avoids standard genre grammar and gives the film a strange, unstable pulse.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    As a late-1990s Taiwanese art film, The Hole matters less for influence than for clarity of voice, it captures urban loneliness, public anxiety, and the collapse of everyday stability with a form that is both local and legible beyond Taiwan. It also stands as an early, memorable example of Tsai’s method, long takes, minimal dialogue, and architecture used as emotional pressure, which helped define his place in contemporary slow cinema.

    💡 Intellectual Depth

    The film is smarter than its plot suggests. The leak between apartments is not just a gimmick, it is the film’s central idea, a literal breach that turns private survival into unwanted contact, and makes loneliness, contamination, and dependence part of the same problem. Its best passages, such as the woman below singing in isolation while the man above watches through the hole, turn ordinary domestic space into a study of human proximity without communication.

    Hyperreal · Film

    • Fri, Sep 11 · 7:30 PM

    A prank comedy with time travel and sharp visual trickery that never sags.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, the film has unusual formal control for something built on prank comedy and time travel nonsense. The reviews point to sharp visual trickery, brisk editing, and a refusal to let the energy sag, which matters in a film that depends on timing, escalation, and the precision of repeated gags.     📚 Cultural Significance, its value lies less in broad social commentary than in showing how a cult object can expand without losing its weird local identity. The film seems to matter as an example of a web-series offshoot that moved into feature length while keeping the same scrappy comic DNA, and the strong critical reception suggests it landed as a real event for fans and a conversion point for newcomers.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, the film’s ideas are clearest in its treatment of arrested adulthood, failed ambition, and the misery of revisiting one’s own past. The New York Times review frames it as a reflective look at paths not taken, and the time-travel setup gives that idea a literal shape, forcing Matt and Jay to confront the younger version of the world they have wasted years circling. It is not philosophically dense, but it does convert a ridiculous premise into a clean argument about regret, friendship, and the comedy of self-sabotage.

    TOWER

    AFS · Film

    • Sun, Sep 13 · 2:00 PM

    Animated testimony reconstructs the UT Tower shooting with kinetic, ugly precision.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, Keith Maitland turns a standard talking head documentary into something far more kinetic, using rotoscope animation, archival material, and survivor testimony to rebuild the University of Texas shooting with ugly precision. The formal device is not decorative, it gives the witnesses faces, rooms, and motion, and it lets the film shift from memory to reenactment without losing momentum.

    ✨ Originality, the film’s strongest move is its refusal to behave like a conventional true crime recap. The animation is not an escape hatch, it is the film’s argument, a way to show the confusion, terror, and split seconds of decision from inside the event rather than from a safe historical distance. That approach is bold, and it keeps the film from slipping into routine tragedy packaging.

    📚 Cultural Significance, Tower matters because it revisits what many critics treat as the first modern mass shooting, and it does so without turning the story into a sermon or a spectacle. Its importance lies in the form as much as the subject, it shows that documentary can carry public history while still inventing a visual language sharp enough to make old testimony feel immediate again.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, the film is most thoughtful when it centers the survivors, not the shooter, and when it treats memory as something unstable but morally urgent. The best passages come from the ordinary details, panic in corridors, hesitation in the face of gunfire, the plain courage of bystanders, and the late-film question of forgiveness, which the film handles as a human complication rather than a slogan.

    • Mon, Sep 7 · 4:05 PM
    • Tue, Sep 8 · 5:00 PM
    • Thu, Sep 10 · 4:45 PM

    Willem Dafoe’s restrained poet finds no redemption in this dry, dialogue-driven drama.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, Kent Jones directs with restraint, and that restraint is the movie’s main virtue. Reviewers consistently note the static, dialogue driven staging, the West Village and SoHo settings, and Willem Dafoe’s controlled turn as Ed Saxberger, a former poet now working in a post office, which gives the film a dry, lived in texture rather than a glossy art world sheen.

    ✨ Originality, The film avoids the usual rise and redemption arc.     📚 Cultural Significance, Late Fame speaks plainly to an era obsessed with recognition, self branding, and the thin line between serious art and performance of seriousness. Its New York milieu, especially the contrast between old bohemia and present day artistic pose, gives it a modest but clear place in the tradition of city movies about failed ambition and late recognition.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, The film’s strongest idea is simple and durable, what does it mean to want recognition after a life of near anonymity, and what is lost when that recognition finally arrives. It gains force from its refusal of grand catharsis, instead letting awkward conversations, social vanity, and Dafoe’s guarded reactions carry the argument.

    Steel Magnolias

    Paramount · Film

    • Wed, Sep 9 · 7:30 PM
    • Thu, Sep 10 · 7:30 PM

    🎭 Artistic Merit Herbert Ross stages Steel Magnolias with a steady hand, and the film’s main strength is its ensemble timing. The beauty shop scenes give the movie its best rhythm, especially the banter between Dolly Parton’s Truvy, Shirley MacLaine’s Ouiser, and Olympia Dukakis’s Clairee, which turns small talk into character portraiture. Sally Field anchors the film with controlled grief, and Julia Roberts gives Shelby a fragile brightness that sharpens the later loss. The film is built for performance, not visual invention, but it knows how to let actors carry the weight.

    ✨ Originality The material is less surprising than the cast’s delivery. It works by contrast, hair spray, sarcasm, and sudden injury, rather than by formal risk. The setting, a Southern beauty parlor in Louisiana, gives the film a distinct social texture, but the structure is conventional, with emotional turns that arrive exactly where expected. Its originality lies in the specificity of the women’s voices, not in narrative design.

    📚 Cultural Significance The film has lasting popular recognition because it made a female friendship circle the center of a mainstream drama and gave several actresses durable showcase roles. Its reputation rests partly on how it packages Southern female resilience, wit, and mourning for a broad audience. That made it widely accessible, though not especially radical.

    💡 Intellectual Depth Steel Magnolias is strongest when it links private sorrow to routine domestic life. The recurring conversations about marriage, illness, work, and motherhood give the film a plainspoken view of dependence, pride, and endurance. It does not probe these ideas with much ambiguity, and the emotional architecture is clear from the start, but the film does understand how ordinary chatter can hide terror, tenderness, and exhaustion.

    Regina Durante Jestrow: Strips, Stripes, Pieces, Parts

    IvesterContemporary · Visual Arts

    • Fri, Sep 11 · 12:00 PM

    Stitched, dyed, and thrifted fabrics form rhythmic geometric puzzles at IvesterContemporary.

    🎭 Formal Qualities, Jestrow’s strength here is structure. The exhibition centers on cut, sewn, and reassembled textile surfaces, with long strips, checkerboard rhythms, and interlacing patterns generating clear visual order rather than decorative drift. The materials carry real friction, dyed, painted, thrifted, and saved fabrics are pressed into compositions that rely on repetition, small shifts, and the hand of assembly, not pictorial illusion.

    ✨ Originality, The work is not inventing textile abstraction from scratch, but it is sharper than routine quilt-adjacent patterning. Jestrow’s method of treating fabric as paint, then breaking it into strips and rebuilding it, gives the show a disciplined push-pull between improvisation and control. That approach keeps the exhibition from settling into craft nostalgia, even if the basic grammar of geometry and textile collage is now well established.

    📚 Cultural Significance, The show lands in current conversations about fiber art, women’s labor, and the politics of quilt traditions. The gallery framing stresses activist values in historic quilt-making, while a Texas arts feature notes Jestrow’s links to women’s rights, domestic history, and cultural diversity, which gives the exhibition more than decorative appeal. Still, the institutional footprint is modest, and the work’s critical reach depends on viewers already invested in textile abstraction.

    💡 Historical Context, Jestrow’s practice is clearly rooted in quilt history, especially American traditions of patchwork, repetition, and domestic labor, but she pushes that lineage toward abstraction and personal symbolism. The residency at MASS MoCA suggests a mature phase of development, and this exhibition reads as a continuation of a long investigation rather than a break with prior work. It sits comfortably between craft history and contemporary geometric painting, though it does not yet force a new category.

    TREES LOUNGE

    AFS · Film

    • Sat, Sep 12 · 4:15 PM

    Steve Buscemi’s unsentimental

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    Steve Buscemi directs with a plain, unsentimental eye, and that restraint gives Trees Lounge its force. The film’s best scenes are small, exact, and bruising, especially the bar-room exchanges where Tommy, played by Buscemi, drifts between self-pity, comic bravado, and sudden shame.
