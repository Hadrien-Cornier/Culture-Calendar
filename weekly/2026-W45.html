    THE GOOD, THE BAD AND THE UGLY

    AFS · Film

    • Tue, Nov 3 · 6:30 PM
    • Fri, Nov 6 · 8:30 PM
    • Sun, Nov 8 · 1:30 PM

    Leone's epic western where greed, honor, and violence meet in a dusty cemetery.

    🎭 Artistic Merit. Leone turns duration into a weapon. The opening takes its time introducing Tuco, Blondie, and Angel Eyes as comic predators, then expands their pursuit across deserts, prisons, battlefields, and a ruined cemetery. Tonino Delli Colli’s widescreen images alternate between vast landscapes and extreme closeups, making geography and facial expression equally oppressive. The bridge sequence, the Union army’s meaningless slaughter, and the cemetery duel show a director who treats spectacle as choreography rather than decoration. Ennio Morricone’s score supplies the film’s nervous system, especially in the cemetery, where editing, glances, hands, and musical rhythm converge. The episodic middle section is overextended, but the control of scale, sound, and suspense remains extraordinary.

    ✨ Originality. The film dismantles the clean moral architecture of the classical Western. Blondie is calculating, Tuco is grotesque and vulnerable, Angel Eyes is efficient rather than mythic, and none of them fits a stable heroic category. Leone combines slapstick cruelty, Civil War carnage, operatic suspense, and grotesque closeup photography without smoothing their contradictions. The final three way duel, with its circular staging and escalating insert shots, converts a familiar gunfight into a ritual of calculation. Its innovations became defining conventions of the revisionist Western, even as later imitators often copied only the poses and music.

    📚 Cultural Significance. The film helped establish the international stature of the Italian Western and permanently altered the genre’s visual vocabulary. Its stylized violence, moral ambiguity, and satire of Western mythology challenged the polished heroics of Hollywood tradition. Its influence extends through filmmakers including Martin Scorsese, Robert Zemeckis, Sam Raimi, and Robert Rodriguez. The movie’s reach is also unusually durable, with its images, character types, music, and cemetery showdown repeatedly cited across popular culture. Its status is earned less by historical realism than by the force with which it made artificiality into a style.

    💡 Intellectual Depth. The film’s ideas emerge through action rather than speeches. The Civil War sections expose official violence as absurd mass slaughter, while the gold hunt reduces loyalty, nationalism, and friendship to temporary bargains. The title’s moral labels are deliberately unstable, since every protagonist behaves according to appetite, calculation, or survival. Tuco’s comic suffering complicates the film’s cynicism, and Blondie’s occasional mercy prevents the narrative from collapsing into pure nihilism. The weakness is that its characters remain primarily theatrical figures, not psychologically developed subjects. Yet the film’s central argument is clear and durable, the frontier is not a realm of moral order, but a marketplace where violence, performance, and chance determine value.

    9 / 10

    FAUST with a live score by The Invincible Czars

    AFS · Film

    • Wed, Nov 4 · 8:00 PM

    Murnau's Faust, live-scored by The Invincible Czars, makes studio craft cosmic.

    🎭 Artistic Merit Murnau’s Faust turns studio construction into a system of visual argument. Mephisto’s wings spread over the plague stricken village, smoke rolls across miniature rooftops, and Faust’s laboratory shifts between cramped human space and cosmic theater. The film’s chiaroscuro, layered compositions, model work, matte effects, and controlled camera movement give abstract forces physical weight. The Invincible Czars’ live score adds a volatile modern counterpoint, combining classical instruments, electric guitar, drums, bossa nova, and metal without merely imitating silent film accompaniment. The film’s weakness is structural. Its early apocalypse and later Gretchen melodrama do not always belong to the same dramatic scale, and the romance sometimes reduces moral conflict to sentimental redemption.

    ✨ Originality The film’s imagery remains startling because it treats special effects as theology rather than decoration. Mephisto becomes a landscape, plague becomes smoke, and temptation becomes a change in visual space. Murnau’s elaborate sets and repeated studio experimentation expanded what cinematic illusion could accomplish, influencing later effects practice. The film does not avoid cliché at the level of plot. It inherits the pact with the Devil, the fallen woman, the innocent child, and the redemptive kiss. Its originality lies in how aggressively it reorganizes those familiar elements through scale, lighting, miniatures, and visual metamorphosis.

    📚 Cultural Significance Faust stands as a major achievement of German Expressionist cinema and a key example of silent film using cinematic form to compete with literature, theater, and painting. Its effects work and studio methods affected subsequent filmmaking, while its image of Mephisto towering over a town remains a durable model for representing evil as an environmental force. The live performance by The Invincible Czars also demonstrates the film’s unusual adaptability. Their score does not preserve the work as a museum piece, it reopens its visual rhythm for a contemporary audience.

    💡 Intellectual Depth The film’s central conflict is not simply good versus evil. Faust seeks knowledge, protection, youth, sexual pleasure, and relief from responsibility, then discovers that each gain enlarges the damage around him. The plague makes private ambition public, while Gretchen’s tragedy exposes the cost of treating another person as evidence of one’s own redemption. Murnau’s final emphasis on love offers moral closure, but it also narrows the film’s most interesting questions about freedom, guilt, and self deception. Even so, the collision between Christian cosmology, erotic appetite, scientific desire, and theatrical spectacle gives the film greater complexity than its melodramatic plot initially suggests.

    CENTER STAGE

    AFS · Film

    • Thu, Nov 5 · 9:00 PM
    • Sat, Nov 7 · 1:30 PM

    Maggie Cheung channels a silent star through fractured film and rehearsal.

    🎭 Artistic Merit Stanley Kwan builds the film from competing textures, reconstructed scenes, surviving silent footage, interviews, rehearsals, and direct address. The switches between Ruan Lingyu’s 1930s performances and Maggie Cheung’s modern preparation prevent the biopic from settling into polished historical pageantry. Cheung’s control is exceptional, especially in the scenes drawn from The Goddess, where her restrained gestures and lowered gaze turn imitation into interpretation. The 154 minute length occasionally loosens the dramatic pressure, but the formal design remains precise and demanding.

    ✨ Originality The film rejects the standard rise and fall biography. Kwan repeatedly exposes the machinery of remembrance, showing actors, researchers, and the director constructing Ruan while questioning how much of her can still be recovered. His own presence complicates the authority of the account, and Cheung’s dual position as actress and investigator makes the film’s central performance unstable in the most productive way. The documentary fiction hybrid feels structurally necessary, not decorative.

    📚 Cultural Significance Center Stage restores Ruan to more than the status of a tragic celebrity. It connects her career to Shanghai’s silent cinema, the politics of representation, the tabloid culture that pursued her, and Hong Kong cinema’s own anxiety about inheritance. By placing Ruan’s surviving images beside Cheung’s reenactments, Kwan makes film history a living argument between generations. Cheung’s performance also became a major landmark in Hong Kong acting, winning her the Berlin Best Actress award, the first such honor awarded to a Chinese woman.

    💡 Intellectual Depth The film asks whether an actress can be separated from the images, gossip, and male narratives that consume her. Ruan’s suicide is not treated as a tidy tragic climax. Instead, Kwan presents it as the result of pressures reproduced by studios, newspapers, intimate relationships, and later filmmakers. The film’s deepest insight lies in its refusal to claim final access to Ruan. Every reconstruction reveals both her presence and the distance separating the present from her life. That uncertainty gives the film its intellectual force.

    THE HISTORY OF CONCRETE

    AFS · Film

    • Mon, Nov 2 · 4:15 PM
    • Tue, Nov 3 · 5:15 PM
    • Wed, Nov 4 · 6:20 PM

    A sly, deadpan comic essay using concrete's history to map American absurdity.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, John Wilson turns a ridiculous premise into a sturdy comic structure, using the search for concrete’s history as a way to move through sidewalks, monuments, city labor, and the low-grade absurdity of American public space. The film’s craft seems strongest in its patient accumulation of odd encounters and visual digressions, with the opening idea, a Hallmark-style pitch for a concrete documentary, giving the piece a sly formal engine rather than a mere gimmick.

    ✨ Originality, this is not a standard documentary, and it gains force from that refusal. The concept bends between satire, essay film, and city symphony, then keeps mutating as Wilson drifts from a commercial pitch exercise into questions about urbanism, memory, and the texture of ordinary life. That shape is uncommon enough to feel genuinely alive, and the comedy comes from structure, not just punch lines.

    📚 Cultural Significance, the film matters as a feature debut that extends Wilson’s TV practice into a longer form without sanding off its peculiar voice. Its subject, concrete, is a blunt material with huge civic reach, and the film uses that fact to think about New York, public life, and the anonymous architecture people move through every day. It may not alter documentary history, but it clearly belongs in the recent wave of essayistic nonfiction that treats the overlooked as a record of culture.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, beneath the comic premise, the film appears to build a serious argument about how cities are made, how value attaches to materials, and how human life leaves marks in supposedly inert things. The best reports on the film describe it moving from joke to reflection without breaking its comic rhythm, with concrete becoming a route into labor, mortality, and the gap between what is built and what is remembered. That is not empty cleverness, it is a coherent idea with enough range to support a full feature.

    NAZA

    AFS · Film

    • Mon, Nov 2 · 4:40 PM

    Concealed Israeli insiders testify on Tel Aviv rooftops in this taut, controlled documentary.

    🎭 Artistic Merit – Shot on Tel Aviv rooftops at night, the film builds its case through 24 concealed interviews with Israeli military and intelligence insiders. Shadowed faces and altered voices turn testimony into a controlled visual form, while the restrained setup keeps attention on the speakers’ accounts and the systems they describe. Its narrow, interview-led approach limits visual variety, but the formal choice suits a film built around protected witnesses.

    ✨ Originality – The film makes bureaucratic language its central device. “Naza,” the projected civilian death toll attached to a strike, converts euphemism into a measure of institutional thinking; the rooftop setting also places the machinery of remote warfare against the ordinary nighttime life of Tel Aviv.

    📚 Cultural Significance – By assembling insider testimony about AI-assisted targeting and civilian deaths in Gaza, NAZA contributes evidence and a record to an urgent political debate. Its public reach is substantial, though impact should not be confused with artistic achievement.

    💡 Intellectual Depth – Its strongest question is how responsibility changes when surveillance, algorithms, military procedure, and individual decisions converge. The testimony challenges claims that civilian deaths are incidental, while the film’s reliance on concealed sources makes the credibility and limits of testimony part of the work’s own argument.

    THE DEVILS

    AFS · Film

    • Mon, Nov 2 · 5:00 PM
    • Tue, Nov 3 · 4:45 PM
    • Wed, Nov 4 · 7:15 PM

    Ferocious, diseased spectacle of possession and execution, anchored by Reed's Grandier.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, Ken Russell stages The Devils with ferocious control of image and tempo, turning historical atrocity into a sequence of hard, ugly set pieces that rarely sit still. The white, skeletal set for Loudun, the mass possession scenes, and the final execution all show a director who understands how to make spectacle feel diseased rather than decorative. Oliver Reed’s Grandier gives the film a physical center, swaggering, bruised, and finally cornered, while Vanessa Redgrave’s Sister Jeanne makes hysteria feel organized, not random.

    ✨ Originality, this is one of the strangest major studio films of its era, and it still looks uncaged. Russell pushes sacred imagery, political satire, sexual repression, and operatic excess into the same frame, and the result is unstable in a way most period horrors never risk. The convent sequences, especially the “possession” material and the later public humiliation, refuse polite genre logic and keep shifting from grotesque comedy to terror to accusation.

    📚 Cultural Significance, the film remains a key case study in censorship, blasphemy panic, and the limits of British screen permissiveness. Its notoriety has lasted because it attacked institutions rather than merely offending taste, and because its production design and performance style gave that attack real cinematic force. The film’s reputation as one of Russell’s defining works is justified by how often later critics still return to it when discussing controversial art cinema.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, beneath the provocation is a clear argument about power. Grandier is destroyed not just by lust or clerical corruption, but by a machinery that turns private desire into public spectacle and uses religious language to cover political violence. The film is strongest when it links sexual panic, institutional cruelty, and mass gullibility, though its ideas are delivered with such racket and frenzy that subtlety is not its goal.

    WILD AT HEART

    AFS · Film

    • Mon, Nov 2 · 6:30 PM
    • Tue, Nov 3 · 7:30 PM
    • Wed, Nov 4 · 4:15 PM
    • Wed, Nov 4 · 8:45 PM
    • Thu, Nov 5 · 5:00 PM

    Lynch's lurid rockabilly road romance: Cage and Dern flee chaos in saturated style.

    🎭 Artistic Merit – Lynch turns a lovers-on-the-run plot into a collision of rockabilly romance, lurid violence, and road-movie excess. Frederick Elmes’s saturated images and Angelo Badalamenti’s uneasy score give the film a firm visual and sonic identity; Nicolas Cage and Laura Dern commit fully to its unstable register. The parade of grotesques sometimes feels more like provocation than dramatic development, and the film’s volume can flatten its emotional shifts.

    ✨ Originality – The film’s most distinctive move is to make familiar Americana feel both absurd and dangerous. Sailor’s snakeskin jacket, the recurring Wizard of Oz imagery, and the abrupt shifts from sex and comedy to brutality build a volatile tone rather than a conventional crime romance. That inventiveness is real, though Lynch occasionally pushes the grotesque until it risks becoming a mannerism.

    📚 Cultural Significance – The Palme d’Or at Cannes marked the film as a major, divisive work in Lynch’s career. Its mix of pulp romance, surreal interludes, and American pop iconography remains a clear example of how far a genre film can bend without abandoning its narrative spine.

    💡 Intellectual Depth – Beneath the spectacle, the film tests whether love can survive a world shaped by violence, family abuse, and trauma. Lula’s memories give the road journey a psychological cost, while the Wizard of Oz references set dreams of escape against the film’s brutal reality. The ideas are sharper than the plot’s loose construction, which prefers feverish episodes to sustained moral inquiry.

    CARRIE

    AFS · Film

    • Mon, Nov 2 · 7:00 PM

    De Palma's prom bloodbath turns adolescent humiliation into a ruinous spectacle.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, De Palma gives the material real formal punch, especially in the prom sequence, where the slow buildup, split focus, and sudden blood dump turn adolescent humiliation into a coordinated crash of image and sound. The final stretch, Carrie’s return home, her mother’s knife attack, and the house collapsing into fire and debris, shows a director who knows how to escalate a scene past shock into ruin. Piper Laurie’s performance adds hard-edged theatrical force, while Sissy Spacek keeps Carrie’s shy, watchful fragility believable enough to make the violence land.

    ✨ Originality, The film avoids generic teen-horror routine by binding telekinesis to embarrassment, religion, and sexual panic, then making the prom feel less like a set piece than a social execution. The ending, with Sue’s nightmare of Carrie’s hand bursting from the grave, became one of horror’s most copied jolts because it is simple, brutal, and perfectly timed.

    📚 Cultural Significance, Carrie became a landmark horror title and one of the defining films of 1976, widely praised on release and still treated as a key Stephen King adaptation. Its influence is visible in later school-horror, revenge, and prom-night nightmare stories, and its image of the blood-soaked girl at the center of a crowd has become part of the genre’s visual vocabulary.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, The film’s ideas are plain but not thin, it studies cruelty as a group act, shame as a trigger, and faith as a weapon when filtered through Carrie’s mother. What gives it weight is the clash between private humiliation and public spectacle, a social mechanism made literal when the prank at the prom turns a teenage outcast into an agent of destruction.

    BEGOTTEN

    AFS · Film

    • Mon, Nov 2 · 7:45 PM
    • Wed, Nov 4 · 4:30 PM

    Wordless creation myth in grainy black-and-white, a relentless assault on film grammar.

    ★ Rating: [8/10]

    🎭 Artistic Merit, Merhige makes the film on a shoestring into an act of visual sabotage, with grainy black and white imagery, no dialogue, and sound built from wind, breathing, water, and animal noise. The result is crude in the best sense, a rigorously controlled assault on conventional film grammar, though its severity can feel willfully punishing rather than fully controlled.

    ✨ Originality, This is the film’s strongest asset. Its creation myth structure, the death, dismemberment, and rebirth imagery, and the refusal of normal narrative make it hard to mistake for anything else. The distorted monochrome look, described by critics as closer to silent-era expressionism and underground moving-image art than horror convention, gives it a damaged, antique texture that still feels alien.

    📚 Cultural Significance, Begotten has remained a reference point for extreme experimental horror, especially for filmmakers interested in mixing religious allegory, body horror, and formal deprivation. Its reputation is larger than its audience, but that is part of its role, it proved that American independent cinema could push past story and character into pure image stress.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, The film is thin on explanation and heavy on symbolic aggression, but that does not make it empty. Its cycle of destruction, violation, and rebirth points toward a bleak cosmology about nature, sacrifice, and the collapse of divine authority, with enough ambiguity to invite interpretation without collapsing into it.

    LA BOLA NEGRA

    AFS · Film

    • Thu, Nov 5 · 5:35 PM
    • Thu, Nov 5 · 8:00 PM
    • Fri, Nov 6 · 5:00 PM
    • Fri, Nov 6 · 8:00 PM
    • Sat, Nov 7 · 5:00 PM
    • Sat, Nov 7 · 8:30 PM
    • Sun, Nov 8 · 2:00 PM
    • Sun, Nov 8 · 5:15 PM

    Three generations, one black ball: desire, violence, and family secrets collide.

    🎭 Artistic Merit Calvo and Ambrossi build a demanding three-part structure across 1932, 1937, and 2017, linking private desire to political violence and family inheritance. The black-ball voting ritual at Carlos’s elite club gives the film a strong visual emblem, while Sebastián’s wartime story turns repression into immediate physical danger. The contemporary plot, centered on Alberto’s investigation and his conflict with his mother, supplies the connective tissue. At 159 minutes, the film occasionally overstates its emotions, but its scale, ensemble casting, and movement between theatricality and historical drama show substantial control.

    ✨ Originality The film’s best idea is not simply to adapt Federico García Lorca’s unfinished play, but to treat its absence as a structural problem. Three gay men, separated by decades, become fragments of a missing history, and the narrative gradually reconstructs meaning through echoes rather than direct explanation. That design avoids a standard chronological account of queer persecution. The result is ambitious and often unpredictable, though the film’s maximalist gestures sometimes announce their symbolism too clearly.

    📚 Cultural Significance La bola negra places queer Spanish experience inside the country’s overlapping histories of class privilege, civil war, dictatorship, family silence, and public memory. Its connection to Lorca gives the project unusual literary weight, while the presence of first-time performers and musicians, including Guitarricadelafuente, broadens its sense of cultural inheritance. The film contributes to Spanish cinema by presenting homosexuality not as a single struggle for acceptance, but as a historical continuity shaped by concealment, violence, art, and survival.

    💡 Intellectual Depth The film’s central question concerns what later generations owe to lives that could not speak openly. Carlos’s exclusion from the club, Sebastián’s wartime displacement, and Alberto’s attempt to decipher his grandfather’s legacy form an argument about memory as reconstruction rather than recovery. Sexual identity, desire, and inheritance remain inseparable from institutions that classify people as acceptable or disposable. The film is strongest when it lets those ideas emerge through objects, rituals, and family disputes. It is weaker when dialogue explains what the structure has already made clear. Even so, the three timelines produce genuine complexity, and the film earns its score above 5 through formal ambition, historical reach, and sustained thematic connection.

    NYRB Book Club - G.

    AlienatedMajesty · Books

    • Sat, Nov 7 · 7:00 PM

    Berger’s picaresque studies desire against the Milan workers’ revolt and Garibaldi’s legacy.

    G. is a sharp, restless novel that turns a seduction plot into a study of appetite, politics, and historical change. Berger gives the book real force by setting G.’s erotic career against the Milan workers’ revolt of 1898, Garibaldi’s legacy, the Boer War, and the first flight across the Alps, so private desire keeps brushing against public history.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, 8/10. Berger’s prose, at least in the book’s own description and critical summaries, aims for compression and range, moving from sex scenes to political upheaval without losing control of the thread. The structure leans picaresque, and that suits the material, because G. is less a rounded psychological portrait than a figure carried through encounters, arguments, and historical crosscurrents.

    ✨ Originality, 8/10. The novel takes the Don Juan figure and strips him of glamour, making him a modern, almost schematic wanderer whose appeal lies in motion, not romance. That approach gives the book a clean conceptual edge, even when the form is closer to literary experiment than to traditional realism.

    📚 Cultural Significance, 8/10. G. won the 1972 Booker Prize, which places it squarely in the postwar English novel canon. Berger’s standing as a novelist and critic also matters here, because the book sits at the junction of fiction, political thought, and art criticism.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, 8/10. The book is interested in how intimacy works inside history, not outside it, and that gives its sexual episodes a social charge. Its best claim is not novelty of subject, but the way it makes pleasure, loneliness, and class movement part of the same argument.

    G. is strong, and at its best more searching than polished. It can feel schematic in its title figure, but the intelligence of its design, and the way it ties appetite to epoch, earns it a high mark.

    WILD STYLE

    AFS · Film

    • Tue, Nov 3 · 4:15 PM

    Raw 1983 hip-hop landmark: graffiti, rap, and street energy, clumsy but alive.

    🎭 Artistic Merit, Charlie Ahearn’s film is rough, sometimes clumsy, and often plays like a collision of staged scenes and street footage, but that rawness is part of its force. The performances from Lee Quinones, Patti Astor, Fab 5 Freddy, Lady Pink, Grandmaster Flash, and the Cold Crush Brothers give it a lived-in texture that polished fiction rarely reaches, and the rap and graffiti sequences have real charge.

    ✨ Originality, The film’s biggest strength is its refusal to behave like a normal narrative. It treats Bronx street culture, tagging, battle rhymes, and breakdance energy as the main event, not decoration, and that choice gives it a jagged, high-risk shape that still feels fresh. The plot is thin, but the film’s improvisatory structure and use of real figures from the scene keep it from sliding into routine.

    📚 Cultural Significance, This is an early, essential document of hip-hop’s birth years, and its value extends beyond nostalgia. It helped carry graffiti and rap into a wider public view, and its scenes of trains, walls, park jams, and the final stage performance preserve a city culture that was still defining itself.

    💡 Intellectual Depth, The film is not intellectually dense in a literary sense, but it does have a clear idea, culture is made in public, by crews, surfaces, and sound, not by institutions. Its best passages show artistic identity as something built through competition, style, and visibility, with the art form itself carrying the argument.

    shapeshifting

    Unchained.Art Contemporary Gallery · Visual Arts

    • Thu, Nov 5 · 1:00 PM
    • Fri, Nov 6 · 12:00 PM
    • Sat, Nov 7 · 12:00 PM

    Industrial materials shift with light in Boley's shape-changing paintings.

    🎭 Formal Qualities

    Boley gives painting a partly industrial body. Cut and sanded metal, reflective vinyl, ink glazes, acrylic, linen, and wood panel create surfaces that alternate between abrasion, opacity, and glare. The process is materially convincing, especially in Shapeshifter, where reflective vinyl makes the image change with the viewer’s position. The available descriptions establish a strong interest in rupture and replacement, but provide too little visual documentation to judge the exhibition’s overall composition, installation, or consistency. The technique is more compelling than the stated metaphor.

    ✨ Originality

    The exhibition’s central idea, identity as something capable of holding several states at once, is familiar. Boley’s stronger contribution lies in forcing that idea through resistant materials. The cut metal and reflective surfaces do not merely illustrate transformation, they make the viewer experience instability through changing reflections and interrupted planes. Still, the conceptual language of tension, change, and selfhood remains broad. The work earns its score through material decisions, not through a radically new premise.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    As Boley’s first solo exhibition, shapeshifting introduces an Austin painter working outside the clean separation between painting and object. Its concern with mutable identity fits contemporary art’s continuing attention to self construction, visibility, and unstable categories. The gallery’s local and international program gives the show a useful institutional setting, but the available evidence does not support claims of major critical impact or a decisive intervention in current discourse. Its significance is presently regional and emergent.

    💡 Historical Context

    Boley extends a lineage of painting that treats the picture surface as a physical structure rather than a transparent window. The combination of metal, sanding, reflective material, and layered paint recalls postwar material abstraction, shaped painting, and later practices that make the viewer’s body part of the image. Her earlier appearance in the group exhibition between us places this solo show at an early career turning point, but there is not enough documented work to map a substantial artistic trajectory. shapeshifting is a strong debut, with real formal promise and an explanatory framework that needs greater specificity.

    WOMEN LAUGHING with Special Guests

    AFS · Film

    • Sat, Nov 7 · 3:30 PM

    Seven women cartoonists draw, laugh, and discuss their craft at AFS.

    🎭 🎭 Artistic Merit

    The film has a clear formal advantage, it lets cartoonists explain their methods while drawing, laughing, and responding to one another. The conversations with Roz Chast, Emily Flake, Liana Finck, Sarah Akinterinwa, Amy Hwang, Bishakh Som, and Emma Allen give the short a useful range of voices and working styles. Its archival movement from the early history of The New Yorker to contemporary cartooning supplies structure, while the group roundtable gives the film an energetic ensemble finish. At 37 minutes, however, the film can only sketch its historical argument. Its craft is efficient and engaging, but not formally adventurous.

    ✨ ✨ Originality

    The strongest idea is to treat drawing itself as testimony. The artists do not merely discuss women’s humor, they demonstrate how an image, caption, or social observation becomes a finished cartoon. That process gives the documentary more texture than a conventional succession of talking heads. Still, the film’s celebratory documentary format, historical overview, individual interviews, and roundtable discussion are familiar. Its originality lies in the access and participants, not in a radically new documentary language.

    📚 📚 Cultural Significance

    The film makes a useful contribution by documenting women cartoonists in a field historically dominated by men and by connecting current artists with overlooked predecessors. Its attention to the first female cartoon editor at The New Yorker, Emma Allen, broadens the subject beyond individual talent to include institutional power. The cultural value is substantial, though the film’s likely impact is archival and corrective rather than transformative. It records a shift in representation while also acknowledging that the field’s remaining obstacles have not disappeared.

    💡 💡 Intellectual Depth

    The documentary raises serious questions about who is permitted to be funny, whose social observations receive institutional legitimacy, and how humor can expose gendered assumptions. Its strongest intellectual material comes from the contrast between the magazine’s male-dominated history and the varied practices of contemporary women cartoonists. Yet the short’s affirmative tone limits its analysis. It identifies exclusion more readily than it examines the economics, editorial systems, and aesthetic conflicts that sustain it. The result is a smart, worthwhile introduction, but not a fully developed account of gender, power, and comedy.

    2026 Austin Polish Film Festival Day 2

    AFS · Film

    • Fri, Nov 6 · 7:30 PM

    🎭 Artistic Merit

    This is a festival program, not a single film, so its artistic value depends on curation rather than unified direction. Day 2 pairs Alex Storozynski’s 54 minute historical documentary, Kościuszko: A Man Before His Time, with Janusz Zaorski’s 2026 drama comedy, Picasso’s Mermaid. The contrast between documentary portraiture and speculative historical comedy gives the evening a workable shape, but no evidence supports judging the program as a formally integrated artwork.

    ✨ Originality

    The strongest idea belongs to Picasso’s Mermaid, in which a mermaid drawn by Picasso on a Warsaw apartment wall disrupts Mary’s life and revives an elderly neighbor’s memories of the artist’s 1948 visit. That premise combines domestic space, visual art, memory, and historical reconstruction, avoiding a routine biographical format. The Kościuszko documentary appears more conventional, built around a historical figure and familiar ideals of freedom, equality, and human rights.

    📚 Cultural Significance

    The program links Polish history to American history through Kościuszko’s role in the American Revolution, then turns to Poland’s artistic memory through Picasso’s Warsaw visit. It offers a compact account of how Polish identity is shaped by political ideals, foreign encounters, and contested remembrance. Its contribution is cultural and curatorial rather than formally groundbreaking.

    💡 Intellectual Depth
